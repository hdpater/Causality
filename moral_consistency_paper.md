# On the Apparent Inconsistencies of Everyday Moral Belief: Partiality, Inheritance, and Taxation

## Abstract

Many people hold, simultaneously, that all persons matter equally, that they are nonetheless entitled to favour their own family and friends; that inherited wealth is unfair, but that providing for one's children is not; and that they support high taxation while having no wish to donate money to the state voluntarily. These pairings look, at first glance, like simple inconsistencies — cases where a general principle is affirmed and then quietly exempted for oneself. This paper argues that each pairing has been the subject of sustained philosophical analysis, and that in each case a principled distinction is available that dissolves *most*, though not all, of the apparent contradiction. It surveys the relevant literature on partiality and impartiality in ethics, on the family's place in a theory of equal opportunity, on the moral difference between coercive and voluntary redistribution, on whether an individual's action can be justified once its actual causal contribution is negligible (the ethics of inconsequentialism), and on how moral philosophy treats inconsistency in ordinary belief more generally (reflective equilibrium, prima facie duties, and the psychology of moral judgement). The conclusion is not that the reader's intuitions are simply vindicated, but that they track real distinctions that professional ethical theory has independently converged on — while some of the tension is genuine, unresolved, and still argued over by philosophers today.

---

## 1. Introduction

The three cases the reader describes share a structure. In each, a general, universalist-sounding commitment (to equality, to fairness in the distribution of opportunity, to redistribution) coexists with a particular, self-favouring exception (love of one's own family, provision for one's own children, reluctance to give away one's own money). It is tempting to call this hypocrisy. But moral philosophy has a long history of asking whether such pairings are *actually* inconsistent, or whether they only look that way because "equality," "fairness," and "support" are being used loosely to cover several distinct ideas that pull apart under pressure. This paper works through the theory relevant to each of the three cases in turn (Sections 2–4), before turning to the more general question of what moral inconsistency even is, and how much of it a reasonable person should expect to live with (Section 5).

## 2. Partiality Toward Family and Friends vs. Belief in Equality

### 2.1 The problem in its classical form

The tension between impartial morality and personal attachment is one of the oldest problems in ethical theory. Henry Sidgwick, in *The Methods of Ethics* (1874), distinguished "Universalistic Hedonism" (utilitarianism proper, which weighs everyone's happiness equally) from the "Morality of Common Sense," which is shot through with obligations of special closeness — to family, friends, and country — that resist justification on purely impartial grounds. Sidgwick found the tension real and, notoriously, left it partly unresolved in what commentators call the "dualism of practical reason": the demands of egoism (and, by extension, of personal loyalty) and the demands of universal benevolence are both rationally binding, and Sidgwick could not fully reconcile them (Sidgwick, 1874/1907).

### 2.2 Williams: the integrity objection

Bernard Williams sharpened this into a direct challenge to impartialist theories (utilitarian and Kantian alike) in his essay in *Utilitarianism: For and Against* and later "Persons, Character and Morality." His famous example involves a man who can save either his drowning wife or a stranger, and who saves his wife. Williams argues that if the man pauses to ask whether partiality toward his wife is *morally permissible* before acting, he has already had "one thought too many": a fully impartialist justification for saving one's spouse corrodes the very relationship that makes the action intelligible as an expression of love rather than of duty (Williams, in Smart & Williams, 1973; see also the exposition in *Luck, Value, and Commitment: Themes from the Ethics of Bernard Williams*). Williams' point is not that partiality is *excused* by morality, but that a moral theory demanding constant readiness to justify partiality impartially misdescribes what a good human life, with its "ground projects" and commitments, actually looks like.

### 2.3 Wolf: the unattractiveness of moral sainthood

Susan Wolf's "Moral Saints" (*Journal of Philosophy*, 79(8), 1982, pp. 419–439) pursues a related line from a different angle. A person who dedicated every resource of time, money, and attention to the impartial good of all — the "moral saint" — would, Wolf argues, be an unattractive figure, someone whose personality is crowded out by moral perfectionism, at the cost of the particular loves, hobbies, and partial commitments that give a life its shape. Wolf does not conclude that morality is bunk; she concludes that the *popular ideal* of impartial moral sainthood is not one we have reason to want to satisfy, which suggests that ordinary partiality is not simply a moral failing to be regretted.

### 2.4 Railton and Scheffler: two ways of making room for partiality inside a principled theory

Peter Railton, in "Alienation, Consequentialism, and the Demands of Morality" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 13(2), 1984, pp. 134–171), takes the Williams/Wolf worry seriously but tries to answer it *from within* consequentialism. His "sophisticated consequentialist" does not consciously calculate the impartial good at every turn; rather, she cultivates dispositions — including deep partiality toward family and friends — because a person with those dispositions in fact produces better impartial outcomes than a person who is calculatingly impartial at every moment. On this view, partiality is not a departure from the impartial good; it is instrumentally validated by it, at one remove.

Samuel Scheffler takes a different, non-consequentialist route in "Relationships and Responsibilities" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 26(3), 1997, pp. 189–209) and in his earlier work on "agent-centred prerogatives" (*The Rejection of Consequentialism*). Scheffler argues that special obligations to family and friends arise directly out of the *value of the relationships themselves*, not as a derivative or instrumental means to impartial goods. Crucially, he also argues that such partiality is *permitted*, not simply *required to be unlimited*: agent-centred prerogatives let you give extra weight to your own projects and attachments without obligating you to maximize your own or your family's welfare at every turn regardless of cost to others. This is important for the reader's case: it supplies a principled boundary — you may favour your family, but the impartialist commitment to equality still operates as a constraint on how far that favouritism can go (it does not license, say, actively harming strangers to help family).

### 2.5 Parfit: partiality as (sometimes) rationally self-defeating in a useful way

Derek Parfit, in *Reasons and Persons* (Clarendon Press, Oxford, 1984), analyses "self-defeating" theories — theories that, followed directly, produce worse results by their own lights than an indirect strategy would. Purely impartial consequentialism, pursued directly, can be self-defeating in just the way Railton and Williams suggest; the correct response, on a Parfitian reading, is not to reject the impartial criterion but to recognize that partial dispositions can be the impartially-best policy to have. Parfit's early work is generally read as sympathetic to impartiality, while his later *On What Matters* moves further toward accommodating partial reasons as basic — indicating that this is a live, evolving question even for a single major philosopher, not a settled one.

### 2.6 The evolutionary and psychological substrate

Partiality toward kin is not merely a philosophical position; it has a biological explanation. W. D. Hamilton's "The Genetical Evolution of Social Behaviour I & II" (*Journal of Theoretical Biology*, 7, 1964, pp. 1–16 and 17–52) formalized "kin selection": organisms are expected to favour relatives in proportion to genetic relatedness, because helping relatives propagates one's own genes indirectly. This gives an evolutionary account of *why* humans have strong, apparently non-negotiable intuitions favouring family — but it is worth flagging the naturalistic fallacy: that partiality is evolutionarily explicable does not by itself justify it. It only explains why the intuition is so stubborn.

Jonathan Haidt's *The Righteous Mind* (Pantheon, 2012) complements this with Moral Foundations Theory, on which human moral judgement rests on several partially independent foundations — including *care*, *fairness*, and *loyalty*. Haidt's central empirical claim is that moral intuitions ("the elephant") come first and reasoning ("the rider") mostly rationalizes them after the fact. On this reading, the pull toward equality (fairness) and the pull toward favouring one's in-group (loyalty) are two *genuinely distinct* moral foundations, not one being corrupted by the other — which is one psychological explanation for why the "inconsistency" feels so natural and is so hard to argue oneself out of.

### 2.7 Synthesis

The recurring move across this literature is to separate **equal moral status** (the idea that every person's interests matter and deserve equal *consideration*) from **equal treatment in practice** (the idea that one must actually *allocate time, money, and care* equally). Scheffler's agent-centred prerogatives, Railton's sophisticated consequentialism, and Williams' integrity argument all supply reasons why the first can be affirmed while the second is denied, without contradiction — provided the partiality stays within limits set by others' basic claims. The apparent inconsistency in believing in equality while favouring family is, on this view, largely dissolved by noticing that "equality" was never a claim requiring equal *treatment*, only equal *standing*.

## 3. Inherited Wealth vs. Providing for One's Children

### 3.1 Rawls and the family as an obstacle to fair equality of opportunity

John Rawls, in *A Theory of Justice* (Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 1971), makes fair equality of opportunity a core principle of justice: people with similar talent and willingness to use it should have similar prospects of success, independent of the social class into which they are born. Rawls treats the family as part of the "basic structure" of society and therefore, in principle, a proper subject of justice — but he also acknowledges that the family, precisely because parents naturally seek to benefit their own children, is a standing obstacle to realizing fair equality of opportunity in full. Rawls does not resolve this tension so much as accept it as a limit on what justice can practically achieve.

### 3.2 Fishkin's trilemma

James Fishkin, in *Justice, Equal Opportunity, and the Family* (Yale University Press, 1983), makes the tension precise. He argues that three widely-held commitments — the autonomy of the family, equality of opportunity (equal life chances regardless of birth), and selection by merit — cannot all be jointly satisfied even under ideal conditions. Strengthening any two undermines the third. This is directly relevant to the reader's intuition: it explains, structurally, why "inherited wealth is unfair" and "parents should be free to provide for their children" cannot both be pursued without limit — some trade-off is logically unavoidable, and the real question is where to draw it, not whether a line exists.

### 3.3 Nozick's libertarian rejection of the tension

Robert Nozick, in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (Basic Books, 1974), denies that there is a problem to solve in the first place. His entitlement theory holds that a distribution of holdings is just if it arose through just acquisition and just (voluntary) transfer — including bequest — regardless of the resulting pattern of inequality. On this "historical," non-patterned view, taxing inheritance to correct for unequal starting points is itself an injustice, since it overrides individuals' rights to dispose of what they justly hold. Nozick's position represents the strongest form of "no inconsistency" reply available: if you fully accept his premises, both inherited wealth and parental provision are simply exercises of the same property right, and there is no tension to explain away — but accepting this requires rejecting patterned principles of distributive justice altogether, which most of the other theorists below do not do.

### 3.4 Mill's intermediate position: a right to bequeath, not a right to inherit

A more precise answer to the reader's specific intuition can be found earlier, in John Stuart Mill's *Principles of Political Economy* (1848). Mill draws exactly the distinction the reader is groping toward: "the right of bequest, or gift after death, forms part of the idea of private property," but "the right of inheritance, as distinguished from bequest, does not." Mill's proposal was that the *giver's* freedom to leave money to whomever they choose should be respected, but the *recipient's* claim to receive an unlimited windfall should not — he favoured a cap on how much any individual could receive by inheritance, precisely to prevent the "over-enriching [of] a few," while preserving the moral legitimacy of a parent's desire to provide. This is arguably the closest single historical precedent to the reader's own view.

### 3.5 Swift and Brighouse: legitimate vs. illegitimate familial advantage

The most direct contemporary treatment is Harry Brighouse and Adam Swift's *Family Values: The Ethics of Parent-Child Relationships* (Princeton University Press, 2014), building on their earlier article "Parents' Rights and the Value of the Family" (*Ethics*, 116(3), 2006, pp. 471–497). Their central move is to ask *what, specifically, is valuable about the family* that justifies parents having any special rights over children at all. Their answer is that parent-child relationships produce distinctive "familial relationship goods" — the intimacy, authority, and love involved in activities like reading a bedtime story, or transmitting one's own values — which matter both to children's healthy development and to parents' own flourishing, and which cannot be provided in the same way by any other institution. Crucially, they argue this justifies parents' latitude over *relationship goods*, but it does **not** justify a parental right to confer wealth or positional advantage on children — elite private schooling bought to secure a competitive edge, or large financial bequests, are not protected by the same rationale, because they are not intrinsic to the parent-child relationship; they are instrumental transfers of social advantage that compete directly with fair equality of opportunity. This is, in effect, a philosophically worked-out version of exactly the distinction the reader is drawing intuitively: some parental provision is legitimate because of what it is (love, attention, values), while other parental provision (wealth transfer) is not automatically legitimate merely because it flows through the same relationship.

### 3.6 Piketty: the empirical stakes

Thomas Piketty's *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (English translation, Harvard University Press / Belknap, 2014; French original 2013) supplies the empirical urgency behind the "inherited wealth is unfair" half of the intuition. Piketty's central finding is that when the rate of return on capital (r) persistently exceeds the rate of economic growth (g), wealth accumulated through capital (including inherited wealth) tends to grow faster than earned income, concentrating assets in the hands of those who already hold them and pushing societies toward what he calls "patrimonial capitalism" — a regime in which birth, not effort, increasingly determines economic position. This gives a consequentialist, not merely deontological, reason to be specifically wary of *large* inherited fortunes, quite apart from any general objection to parental generosity.

### 3.7 Synthesis

Read together, these sources suggest that the reader's intuition is not an inconsistency at all, but an implicit and fairly sophisticated position that the professional literature has arrived at independently: disaggregate "providing for one's children" into (a) the relationship goods of parenting — attention, values, love, everyday material care — which are close to universally regarded (Rawls, Swift & Brighouse) as legitimate and not properly subject to redistribution, and (b) large, unearned transfers of capital, which are the actual target of "inherited wealth is unfair" and which Mill, Rawls, Fishkin, and Piketty all treat, in different ways, as a legitimate object of limitation or taxation. The disagreement that remains — chiefly with Nozick — is a genuine, first-order dispute in political philosophy about whether patterned principles of distributive justice are defensible at all, not a personal inconsistency on the reader's part.

## 4. Support for High Taxation vs. Reluctance to Donate Voluntarily

### 4.1 The most direct challenge: Singer

Peter Singer's "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 1(3), 1972, pp. 229–243) states the challenge to the reader's third pairing most starkly. Singer argues that if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we are morally obligated to do it — and that this principle, taken seriously, implies an obligation to give away money to relieve suffering far beyond what is legally required, right up to the point of marginal utility. On a strict Singer-style reading, the fact that taxation is compulsory and donation is not should be morally irrelevant: if the money would do more good given away than kept, keep-vs-give is the same question whether or not the state has already taken a share via tax. This is the position that most directly *validates* the sense that something is inconsistent in supporting high taxes while declining to give voluntarily.

### 4.2 Murphy and Nagel: why tax is not a "sacrifice" in the way giving is

The most influential reply is Liam Murphy and Thomas Nagel's *The Myth of Ownership: Taxes and Justice* (Oxford University Press, 2002), extending their earlier "Taxes, Redistribution, and Public Provision" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 30(1), 2001, pp. 53–71). Their central claim is that pre-tax income and holdings are not a moral baseline of entitlement from which taxation then "takes." Property rights themselves are a legal convention that only exists *because of* a system of law backed by the state — including the tax system. On this view, asking "why don't you voluntarily give away money you support taxing?" misdescribes the situation: the tax system is not you *voluntarily surrendering your own resources* in the way a donation is; it is one part of the very system that defines what "your resources" are in the first place. Supporting a just, comprehensive tax-and-transfer system is a matter of institutional design; it is a different kind of act from personal charitable sacrifice, and need not be governed by the same principle.

### 4.3 Murphy's fair-share / institutional-obligation view

Murphy develops the individual side of this dualism further in "Institutions and the Demands of Justice" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 27(4), 1998, pp. 251–291), arguing that principles of justice properly apply differently to institutions and to individual conduct: individuals are not, in general, required to privately compensate for the shortfalls of unjust or incomplete institutions by unilaterally shouldering the whole burden themselves. Rather, individuals owe their *fair share* within a cooperative scheme (like a tax system) that is designed, collectively, to achieve just outcomes. On this reading, wanting the state to tax everyone at a high rate, while not wanting to personally donate on top of one's already-high fair share, is not hypocritical — it is a coherent application of "everyone should do their part in a coordinated scheme," as opposed to "everyone should individually try to solve the whole problem."

### 4.4 Two logics of collective action: depleting a commons and needing assurance

This institutional argument has a structural complement in collective-action theory, which in fact contains at least two distinct mechanisms worth separating.

The first is the free-rider problem proper. Mancur Olson's *The Logic of Collective Action: Public Goods and the Theory of Groups* (Harvard University Press, 1965) explains why voluntary, uncoordinated individual contributions systematically undersupply public goods: each individual has an incentive to free-ride on others' contributions, since the goods (once provided) are available regardless of one's own contribution, and any single person's voluntary gift is a vanishingly small fraction of what is needed. James Andreoni's "Impure Altruism and Donations to Public Goods: A Theory of Warm-Glow Giving" (*The Economic Journal*, 100(401), 1990, pp. 464–477) sharpens this: even a sincere, non-selfish donor under-gives relative to what she would want a *compulsory, universal* scheme to raise, because a private gift buys two things — a shared public benefit, and a private "warm glow" that only the giver gets — and voluntary giving tends to track the second, smaller quantity rather than the first. A single household's private donation to the state cannot solve a redistribution problem the way a compulsory, universal tax scheme can, because taxation as a mechanism exists precisely to *overcome* this under-provision.

The second is the older, resource-depletion version of the same structure: Garrett Hardin's "The Tragedy of the Commons" (*Science*, 162(3859), 1968, pp. 1243–1248) describes a shared, finite resource (Hardin's own example is common grazing land) that each self-interested user has an incentive to over-exploit, because the cost of restraint is borne entirely and immediately by the individual, while the benefit of restraint is shared with, and diluted among, everyone else. This is the more exact model for the reader's car-use example: each additional car journey's contribution to congestion, air pollution, or carbon emissions is negligible on its own, but the aggregate of everyone's individually negligible trips depletes a genuinely shared resource (clean air, uncongested roads, a stable climate). Giving up one's own car costs real, concentrated time and money; the resulting benefit is spread so thinly across millions of other people that it is imperceptible back to the person who paid the cost. A binding collective policy — a fuel duty, a congestion charge, transit investment — changes this calculation for everyone at once, which is exactly why someone can rationally and non-hypocritically support such a policy while continuing, alone, to drive.

It is worth noting a genuine complication here. Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action* (Cambridge University Press, 1990) shows empirically that Hardin's pessimism is not universal: small communities have repeatedly self-organized to manage shared resources — irrigation systems, fisheries, forests — without top-down coercion, given specific conditions (clearly defined boundaries, monitoring, graduated sanctions, cheap local conflict resolution). But those conditions are precisely what large, anonymous societies dealing with national road networks, tax systems, or carbon emissions typically lack; Ostrom's finding does not undermine the case for coercive, state-level solutions to *this* class of problem so much as clarify exactly when voluntary self-governance can substitute for them and when it cannot.

Finally, both mechanisms above are usefully distinguished from a third: the *assurance problem*, formalized by Amartya Sen in "Isolation, Assurance and the Social Rate of Discount" (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 81(1), 1967, pp. 112–124). Sen separates a person's "isolation preference" — what they would rationally choose to do acting alone, with no guarantee about others' behaviour — from their "assurance preference" — what they would choose if guaranteed that everyone else would act likewise. These can differ without any inconsistency, because they answer two different questions. The classical illustration predates Sen by two centuries: Jean-Jacques Rousseau's stag hunt, from the *Discourse on the Origin and Foundations of Inequality Among Men* (1755), describes hunters who could cooperate to catch a stag (feeding everyone well) or break ranks to chase a hare (a smaller but certain reward available to anyone acting alone). Unlike Olson's or Hardin's problems, defection is not the dominant strategy here — cooperating is better for everyone *if* the others cooperate too — but each hunter's rational choice still depends entirely on how much they trust the others not to bolt for the hare. Wanting the stag hunt (a binding collective rule) while declining to be the one who charges the stag alone is not weakness of will; it is the correct response to an assurance game, not a prisoner's dilemma.

Read back onto the tax case from Sections 4.1–4.3: "many of the rich support higher taxes" is close to a textbook instance of Sen's gap between isolation and assurance preference. A single wealthy person's extra voluntary payment, made alone, funds nothing at a scale that matters and leaves that person at a real, private, *relative* disadvantage against peers who did not also pay more — the hare, in effect, taken by everyone else while she alone went for the stag. A binding rule that raises the rate for everyone removes exactly that relative disadvantage, because the comparison class moves too. The same person can, without contradiction, decline the first and vote for the second.

### 4.5 The same pattern beyond taxation: schooling and health care

Private schooling and private health care involve a related but structurally different mechanism, best captured by Albert O. Hirschman's *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty: Responses to Decline in Firms, Organizations, and States* (Harvard University Press, 1970). Hirschman distinguishes two responses to a deteriorating institution: *exit* (leaving it for an alternative) and *voice* (staying and pressing for improvement from within). His key structural claim is that exit and voice tend to substitute for one another rather than coexist, and that the people best equipped to make voice effective — the articulate, well-resourced, well-connected — are usually also the people most able to exit. When those people exit a struggling public school or health system for a private one, the constituency with the most power to improve the public alternative shrinks just as that alternative most needs it, which can make conditions worse for those who cannot exit, which in turn gives the exiters retrospective justification for having left. This is a real feedback loop in the world, not merely an inconsistency in belief: a person's preference for a good universal system and their individual act of exit can both be genuine, and yet still work against one another.

Adam Swift's *How Not to Be a Hypocrite: School Choice for the Morally Perplexed Parent* (RoutledgeFalmer, 2003) takes up the schooling case directly, asking whether it is hypocritical for a parent who supports comprehensive education — even to the point of wanting elite private schools restricted — to send her own child to one. His qualified answer is: not automatically. He distinguishes the political question (what school system justice requires, and what a citizen should advocate and vote for) from the parental question (what a parent may permissibly do for one particular, non-repeatable child under the *current*, imperfect rules) — which is the same distinction Brighouse and Swift draw between legitimate familial relationship goods and illegitimate transfers of advantage (see Section 3.5): a parent's latitude to do right by her own child does not disappear just because the system she is operating inside is one she also opposes. But Swift is explicit that the defence has limits: it weakens sharply if the local state school is not actually bad enough to justify the sacrifice to the child, and it fails entirely if the same parent is also campaigning against the reforms that would make the private option unnecessary.

Health care raises a parallel question with less bespoke philosophical literature but an existing framework that extends naturally to it. Norman Daniels, in *Just Health: Meeting Health Needs Fairly* (Cambridge University Press, 2008), grounds the special claim of health care on justice in its role protecting fair equality of opportunity — the same Rawlsian value at stake in Sections 3.1 and 3.5. On Daniels' framework, some private supplementary care alongside a genuinely adequate public tier need not be unjust, but the Hirschman dynamic still applies with force: large-scale exit into private care by those with the most money and the loudest political voice removes exactly the pressure and the political coalition that would otherwise work to keep the public tier adequate for everyone else. Wishing the public system were good enough that private care were unnecessary, while using private care now, is not therefore a simple contradiction — but it is not costless either: it can be one more instance of the same exit that erodes the very outcome being wished for.

### 4.6 Cohen's rejoinder: the tension is not fully dissolved

G. A. Cohen's *If You're an Egalitarian, How Come You're So Rich?* (Harvard University Press, 2000, based on his 1996 Gifford Lectures) pushes back against exactly this comfortable division of labour. Cohen argues that Rawlsian and similar views let egalitarians off the hook too easily by locating justice entirely in institutional design (the "basic structure"), while permitting self-interested behaviour by individuals within the rules those institutions set. He insists that a serious commitment to equality is not only a matter of what rules you vote for, but also a matter of personal ethos — how you actually behave with your own resources, day to day. Applied directly to the reader's third case, Cohen's argument implies that supporting high taxes while refusing to give more than one is legally compelled to is not fully vindicated by the Murphy/Nagel institutional story; there remains a live question of whether one's own personal conduct, and not only one's preferred policy, should reflect egalitarian commitment. This is best treated as a genuine, unresolved disagreement between serious philosophers (Cohen versus Murphy and Nagel) rather than a settled matter — which means the reader's discomfort here tracks a real fault line in the literature, not a simple error.

### 4.7 Non-governmental mechanisms for collective action

The preceding sections diagnose why unilateral, voluntary action under-supplies collective goods; a further question is whether anything short of state coercion can supply them anyway. The relevant literature answers with two distinct strategies. The first leaves self-interest untouched and rewires the *payoff* structure so that cooperation becomes the rational choice regardless of one's underlying disposition; the second works on the *preference* itself, cultivating a psychology or culture in which the temptation to defect does not arise. Most durable non-governmental mechanisms belong to the first family.

Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990), already introduced in Section 4.4, supplies the fullest catalogue. Studying long-enduring, self-organized irrigation systems, fisheries, and forests that avoided Hardin's tragedy without a central regulator, she distilled eight recurring design principles: clearly defined boundaries on both the resource and its user group; rules tailored to local conditions with the cost of participation kept roughly proportional to its benefit; collective-choice arrangements in which the users themselves can amend the rules; monitoring, typically performed by the users or those accountable to them; graduated sanctions that punish first offenses lightly and escalate on repetition; cheap, fast local mechanisms for resolving disputes; recognized autonomy from outside authorities to organize in this way at all; and, for resources that span a wider area, nested layers of governance rather than a single central rule-maker. What does the work, on this account, is not altruism but visibility and consequence: because each user is monitored by neighbours who depend on the same resource and will remember, defection stops paying.

A second mechanism achieves the same result through repetition rather than local institution-building. Robert Axelrod's *The Evolution of Cooperation* (Basic Books, 1984) reports computer tournaments of the iterated prisoner's dilemma in which the simplest strategy — tit-for-tat, cooperate first and then mirror the opponent's last move — consistently defeated more elaborate rivals. When the same parties expect to interact again, and can condition future behaviour on present conduct, cooperation becomes a self-interested equilibrium rather than a moral achievement: what Axelrod calls "the shadow of the future" makes defection costly through anticipated retaliation. This is the underlying logic of reputation markets that did not exist before the internet made them cheap to build — seller and buyer ratings, host and guest reviews, open-source commit histories, Wikipedia's edit log — each manufactures the conditions of a repeated game and a visible track record for parties who would otherwise interact once and anonymously.

A closely related, older result is Robert Ellickson's *Order Without Law: How Neighbors Settle Disputes* (Harvard University Press, 1991), an empirical study of cattle ranchers in Shasta County, California, who resolved disputes over trespassing livestock through informal norms of neighbourly reciprocity and largely ignored the formal legal rules that technically governed the matter. The mechanism is the same as Axelrod's, operating through a small, stable community rather than a two-player game: repeated interaction and local reputational cost enforce cooperative norms without recourse to courts.

A different route to the same end works through property rights rather than repetition. Ronald Coase, in "The Problem of Social Cost" (*Journal of Law and Economics*, 3, 1960, pp. 1–44), showed that when property rights are clearly assigned and the cost of negotiating is low, the parties affected by an externality can bargain their way to an efficient outcome regardless of who initially holds the right — a factory and a downwind laundry can strike their own deal over smoke damage without a regulator specifying the outcome. Coase's own qualification is decisive for what follows: the result depends on transaction costs being low enough for bargaining to occur at all.

Two further mechanisms work by altering the structure of the good itself. James Buchanan's "An Economic Theory of Clubs" (*Economica*, 32(125), 1965, pp. 1–14) observes that many goods sit between the purely private and the purely public, and that where a good *can* be made excludable, a voluntary club can supply it to members while denying it to non-contributors — mutual insurance pools, cooperatives, homeowners' associations, professional guilds. This does not so much solve the free-rider problem canvassed in Section 4.4 as sidestep it, by converting a public good into a members-only one. Alex Tabarrok's "dominant assurance contracts" ("The Private Provision of Public Goods via Dominant Assurance Contracts," *Public Choice*, 96(3–4), 1998, pp. 345–362) achieve something more precise: a crowdfunding scheme that refunds pledges with a bonus if the funding threshold is not reached makes contributing the individually dominant strategy no matter what anyone else does, engineering away the isolation/assurance gap identified by Sen (Section 4.4) by construction rather than by trust. Kickstarter's ordinary all-or-nothing funding model is a weaker version of the same idea, without the bonus.

The remaining mechanism belongs to the second family — altering preference rather than payoff. Robert Frank, in *Passions Within Reason: The Strategic Role of the Emotions* (W. W. Norton, 1988), argues that emotions such as guilt and a sense of honour evolved specifically as commitment devices: because they are difficult to fake convincingly, a person genuinely disposed to feel guilt at defecting is, for that very reason, a more trustworthy cooperation partner, and that visible trustworthiness is itself a competitive advantage in a reputation-based world. On this account, some people simply do not want to defect, and this disposition — not calculation — does the work that Ostrom's monitoring or Axelrod's retaliation do elsewhere.

Nearly every mechanism above depends on the same underlying conditions: a bounded or excludable membership, so that non-cooperators can be identified and either sanctioned or denied the benefit; interaction that repeats over time, so a reputational or retaliatory future exists to discipline the present; behaviour that is cheap to observe, so monitoring does not itself become prohibitively costly; a low cost of organizing rules and enforcement relative to the value at stake; and some credible mechanism of enforcement, even an entirely informal one, so that agreement is more than cheap talk.

These conditions also mark the limits. They are most fully satisfied in exactly the small-to-medium, geographically or socially bounded settings Ostrom, Ellickson, and Axelrod studied — a few hundred to a few thousand participants who know, or can come to know, one another's conduct. They weaken sharply as groups grow anonymous and mobile: reputational discipline needs a persistent identity to attach to, and modern, large-scale, often one-shot interactions (global trade, anonymous online exchange, a shifting population) erode the "shadow of the future" on which tit-for-tat depends. Coasean bargaining fails for the same underlying reason once the number of affected parties grows large: negotiating a deal between a single polluter and a few million downwind residents is itself a collective-action problem, since someone has to organize the residents' side, which simply relocates rather than resolves the difficulty. Even successful Ostrom-style institutions face a second-order version of the same puzzle — someone must bear the cost of monitoring and sanctioning, and that person's own compliance is, in principle, a further public good; in practice this is resolved by rotating the duty within a small, already-cooperating group, a fix that does not obviously generalize. Club goods and assurance contracts, meanwhile, require a good that can be made excludable or given a clean funding threshold; air quality, climate stability, and herd immunity cannot be fenced off from anyone regardless of contribution, so these mechanisms have nothing to act on. Informal norm-governance can also entrench existing power and exclude newcomers or outsiders who are not part of the reputational network, and offers no protection to anyone the in-group can externalize costs onto. And preference-based mechanisms are the slowest and least deployable of all: culture, evolved commitment emotions, and moral education build up over generations rather than to a policy deadline, and the same reputational logic that sustains them can unravel quickly in the other direction once visible defection becomes common.

The overall shape of the evidence, then, is not that non-governmental mechanisms are illusory, but that they are strongest precisely where the stakes are smallest and most local, and weakest precisely where the largest collective-action problems — climate change, pandemic prevention, biodiversity loss — actually sit. This is the structural reason coercive, state- or treaty-level intervention keeps being reached for at that end of the spectrum: not because voluntary and market mechanisms have failed through some lack of ingenuity, but because the scale, anonymity, and non-excludability of the largest commons problems remove precisely the conditions — visibility, repetition, exclusion, cheap enforcement — that make every mechanism surveyed here work.

### 4.8 Case study: energy and climate as a nested collective-action problem

Energy consumption combines every mechanism examined in this section into a single, unusually hard case, because the collective-action problem is nested two levels deep. At the individual level it resembles Hardin's commons directly (Section 4.4): each person's energy use contributes negligibly to a shared atmospheric resource, so unilateral restraint costs the individual real money and inconvenience for an imperceptible collective benefit. But energy use is also, simultaneously, a collective-action problem *among states*: each country's economy benefits from cheap, abundant energy, while the atmospheric cost is borne globally and diffusely, and any single country's restraint is both individually costly and collectively negligible in exactly Hardin's sense — now with states playing the role of Hardin's herdsmen.

This nesting matters because the standard remedy for a domestic commons problem — a government able to compel compliance, Hardin's own "mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon" — has no analogue at the level where the problem now sits. International relations lacks a central authority empowered to coerce sovereign states the way a state coerces its citizens; each government, moreover, faces its own domestic version of the same asymmetry Sen's isolation/assurance framework identifies (Section 4.4), since unilateral national restraint imposes visible, near-term costs (energy prices, jobs) for a benefit that is diffuse, delayed, and shared with free-riding states, while a government's negotiating room abroad is itself bounded by what it can sell to voters at home — the "two-level game" Robert Putnam describes in "Diplomacy and Domestic Politics: The Logic of Two-Level Games" (*International Organization*, 42(3), 1988, pp. 427–460).

The empirical record nonetheless offers a genuine contrast case. Scott Barrett, in *Environment and Statecraft: The Strategy of Environmental Treaty-Making* (Oxford University Press, 2003), compares the Montreal Protocol on ozone-depleting substances (1987), which succeeded, with the Kyoto Protocol on greenhouse gases, which largely did not. Montreal regulated a small number of chemicals produced by a handful of firms, had available substitutes that kept compliance cheap, and — critically — backed the agreement with trade sanctions against non-signatories, giving it real enforcement without requiring a supranational authority. Kyoto asked for costly, near-universal cuts with no comparable enforcement mechanism, reproducing the free-rider dynamic Olson's theory predicts (Section 4.4): no state had reason to bear the cost of compliance alone.

That contrast identifies the mechanisms that actually have traction where a world government does not exist. William Nordhaus's "Climate Clubs: Overcoming Free-Riding in International Climate Policy" (*American Economic Review*, 105(4), 2015, pp. 1339–1370) shows formally that, absent sanctions, stable international coalitions form only at minimal levels of abatement — but a club of states that price carbon internally and impose even modest trade tariffs on non-members can sustain a large, high-abatement coalition. This is Buchanan's club-goods logic (Section 4.7) applied to states: once remaining outside the club costs more, through tariffs, than joining costs in abatement, membership becomes each state's self-interested choice rather than an act of trust. The European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism is this proposal already being implemented. Elinor Ostrom extended her own commons framework to precisely this problem in "A Polycentric Approach for Coping with Climate Change" (World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 5095, 2009), arguing against waiting for a single global agreement on the grounds that it reproduces the trust problem at the scale where it is hardest to solve; action at nested levels — city, region, sector, nation — can instead be locally self-interested through co-benefits such as cleaner air, energy security, and lower costs, without requiring global enforcement at all, extending the "nested enterprises" design principle already discussed.

A further mechanism dissolves the dilemma rather than managing it. The falling cost of solar power, wind power, and batteries means that in a growing share of applications, clean energy is now simply the cheaper option, independent of any climate policy; where cooperation and self-interest converge on the same choice, defection stops being tempting regardless of trust or enforcement, in the same way Tabarrok's dominant assurance contracts (Section 4.7) remove Sen's isolation/assurance gap by construction rather than by persuasion. This also reframes state-level self-interest directly: leadership in battery and solar manufacturing is an industrial prize competed for on its own economic terms, which is part of why decarbonization is increasingly promoted as competitiveness rather than sacrifice.

These mechanisms work, where they work, by changing what counts as a payoff rather than asking for sacrifice: trade tariffs make non-cooperation costly, falling technology costs make cooperation cheap, and co-benefits are popular independent of any climate rationale. But the case also displays the limits of the whole toolkit examined in this section with unusual clarity. No cheap substitute yet exists for some of the hardest sectors — aviation, shipping, heavy industry, agriculture — leaving the technology-substitution mechanism nothing to act on there. Olson's concentrated-cost, diffuse-benefit asymmetry (Section 4.4) cuts specifically against climate policy: the losers from stranded fossil assets are few, visible, and well organized, while the winners from decarbonization are diffuse and rarely attribute their gains to the policy that produced them, so opposition tends to mobilize harder than the arithmetic of costs and benefits would predict. Climate clubs and border adjustments require enough powerful founding members to make exclusion genuinely costly, and raise real equity objections, since they can effectively tax countries that contributed least to the accumulated stock of emissions and most need cheap energy now. And existing fossil infrastructure has decades-long lifespans, creating lock-in and stranded-asset risk that makes even a net-beneficial transition politically costly in the near term — the same visible-loss-versus-diffuse-gain structure that makes unilateral national restraint electorally unattractive in the first place.

### 4.9 Synthesis

The taxation/donation case is the one of the three where the "inconsistency" survives best-informed scrutiny in a *partial* form. Murphy and Nagel's institutional argument, together with Olson's, Hardin's, Sen's, and Hirschman's distinct collective-action mechanisms, give strong, principled reasons why compulsory, collective arrangements and voluntary, individual action are different in kind — not because one is easy and the other virtuous, but because they solve genuinely different problems (under-provision of a public good, depletion of a shared resource, lack of assurance about others' conduct, and erosion of voice through exit, respectively). This explains why the same underlying preference — for less driving, higher taxes, better state schools, a good public health system — can rationally and non-hypocritically be paired with unilateral behaviour that looks, superficially, like its opposite. But Cohen's ethos-based challenge, and the strict form of Singer's utilitarian argument, show that this defence has limits: neither fully answers the question of how much *personal* sacrifice justice might still demand once the compulsory scheme is in place, or how much weight individual "exit" decisions should carry even when they are individually rational. Philosophers disagree; it would be inaccurate to present the tension as wholly dissolved. Section 4.7's survey of non-governmental mechanisms adds a further qualification: voluntary, market, and community-based alternatives to coercive redistribution are real and sometimes highly effective, but they work by reproducing, informally, the very features — monitoring, repetition, excludability, enforcement — that a compulsory scheme supplies by design, and they weaken in step with the scale and anonymity of the problem. For a collective-action problem the size of national redistribution, this is a reason to expect voluntary substitutes to fall short, not a reason to think the compulsory mechanism is doing something conceptually illegitimate. Section 4.8's case study on energy and climate shows what happens when the problem outgrows even the state: where no authority exists to supply Hardin's "mutual coercion," the mechanisms that make progress are the ones that change payoffs directly — trade-backed clubs and falling technology costs — rather than any mechanism that depends on trust or goodwill at planetary scale.

## 5. Does It Matter If My Action Makes No Difference? The Ethics of Inconsequentialism

### 5.1 The problem stated precisely

Sections 2 through 4 largely took for granted that a mechanism's or an act's justification tracks its causal contribution to some outcome — this is what the isolation/assurance gap (Section 4.4) and the club and assurance-contract mechanisms (Section 4.7) are built to close. A harder question sits underneath all of it. In paradigm cases of everyday ethical action — voting, recycling, buying free-range or plant-based food, cutting personal energy use — the individual's actual causal contribution to the outcome is not merely small but, on the most natural model, exactly zero: no single vote decides a national election under first-past-the-post; no single abstention from meat detectably reduces the number of animals raised and killed; no single household's emissions detectably change the climate. If moral justification runs through consequences, and the consequence of the individual act is genuinely nil, "be the change you want to see" looks less like a serious ethical claim and more like moral theatre. Jonathan Glover's "It Makes No Difference Whether or Not I Do It" (*Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society, Supplementary Volume*, 49, 1975, pp. 171–210) gave this argument its canonical name and traced it through population and poverty cases; it recurs, largely unchanged in structure, in every domain surveyed in this paper.

### 5.2 Denying the premise: expected value

The most direct reply denies that the individual's *actual* contribution is the right thing to compute. Shelly Kagan's "Do I Make a Difference?" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 39(2), 2011, pp. 105–141) observes that markets typically respond to demand in discrete batches rather than continuously — a farmer restocks once orders cross a threshold, not order by order — so while any single purchase is very likely to make no difference at all, it carries a small, calculable probability of being the purchase that tips demand over that threshold. Multiplying that probability by the size of the effect when it does tip yields a nonzero, sometimes substantial, *expected* impact, even though the *actual* impact of nearly every individual instance of the act is zero. The clearest worked example of this logic is electoral. Anthony Downs first posed the puzzle in *An Economic Theory of Democracy* (Harper & Row, 1957): if voting is costly and the probability of being decisive is negligible, a narrowly self-interested voter should not bother. Riker and Ordeshook's "A Theory of the Calculus of Voting" (*American Political Science Review*, 62(1), 1968, pp. 25–42) formalized the decision as R = PB − C + D, where P is the probability of being decisive, B the stakes, C the cost of voting, and D a residual term for civic duty added because P×B alone could not explain observed turnout. What P actually is, computed rather than guessed, turns out to matter a great deal: Gelman, Silver, and Edlin, "What Is the Probability Your Vote Will Make a Difference?" (*Economic Inquiry*, 50(2), 2012, pp. 321–326), calculated real pivotality for the 2008 US presidential election at roughly one in ten million in the most competitive states and about one in sixty million nationally — far smaller than intuition typically assumes, but not zero, and multiplied by stakes affecting the whole population, not obviously negligible in expectation either.

### 5.3 Why the argument works: serial versus parallel causal structure

What makes the expected-value reply available at all is a feature of the underlying causal structure that is easy to miss. The inconsequentialism argument implicitly treats the individual's contribution as one link in a *serial* chain: a sequence of separate events that must each occur for the outcome to depend on the act, so that the probability of the whole chain holding is the product of the probabilities of each link, and shrinks — often rapidly — as the chain lengthens. This is the structure of a series system in reliability engineering, where a system built from components that must all function has a reliability equal to the product of the individual components' reliabilities, and becomes *less* reliable the more components are added. But voting, buying, and emitting are not series systems; they are *parallel* systems — many independent, structurally symmetric contributors, where the outcome depends on how the group's contributions sum or compare against some standard, and any one of them could turn out to be the contributor whose presence is decisive. A parallel system built from redundant components becomes *more*, not less, reliable as components are added. The probability that a given voter is pivotal in a large election is small, but it does not decay by repeated multiplication the way a long conjunctive chain would; it is a combinatorial fact about the distribution of everyone else's votes, and Gelman, Silver, and Edlin's finding that P shrinks roughly as the reciprocal of the electorate while the stakes B grow roughly in proportion to it — so that P×B stays remarkably stable as elections get larger — is exactly the signature of a parallel rather than serial structure. The intuitive pull of "my vote/purchase/emission can't possibly matter" trades on imagining a fragile chain where the correct model is a redundant, parallel aggregation.

A schematic case, offered here as the present author's own device rather than drawn from the literature, may make the distinction vivid. Suppose a boulder requires ten people to shift it, and fifteen turn up. Afterwards, once it has gone over the edge, no individual push was necessary: remove any one of the fifteen and fourteen remain, which is still more than ten, so the boulder moves exactly as it did. Every one of the fifteen can say, with complete accuracy, that it would have happened without them, and all fifteen are right.

But consider the same fifteen the day before, when nobody yet knows how many will turn up. Each now faces a distribution over the possible turnouts. For each individual there is some real and computable probability that exactly nine others arrive, in which case that person's push is the entire difference between the boulder moving and the boulder staying put. The probability is small; it is not zero; and multiplied by what the boulder does when it moves, it yields a genuine expected value.

The "it would have happened without me" claim is thus a *retrospective* fact about a single outcome that has already resolved, while the reason to turn up is a *prospective* fact about a distribution that has not. The inconsequentialism argument trades on substituting the first for the second, using what turned out to be true afterwards to answer a question that can only be posed beforehand.

### 5.4 Generalizing to continuous aggregation: Broome and climate

Kagan's version of the argument depends on markets having a discrete threshold to tip — a feature that seems available for meat and dairy purchases but much less obviously available for atmospheric carbon, which accumulates smoothly rather than through periodic restocking events. This looks, at first, like a genuine limit on how far the expected-value reply generalizes. But recognizing the underlying structure as parallel rather than serial shows the limit is narrower than it appears: a parallel structure does not require a discrete gate to yield real expected value, only that contributions sum toward an outcome whose badness is a function of the sum. John Broome, in *Climate Matters: Ethics in a Warming World* (W. W. Norton, 2012), makes exactly this continuous-case argument: because climate damage is, to a reasonable approximation, proportional to cumulative emissions, an individual's marginal tonne has a calculable, continuous, nonzero expected contribution to harm, given by the slope of the damage function at the current level of emissions, with no threshold-crossing event required at all. Kagan's discrete threshold and Broome's continuous slope are the same underlying move — parallel aggregation yields real expected value — applied to a gated sum and a smooth sum respectively; what defeats the expected-value reply is genuinely serial structure, where the individual's contribution matters only conditional on some further chain of independent events also holding, not the mere absence of a discrete gate.

### 5.5 Denying that difference-making is the right currency

A more radical reply gives up the difference-making standard itself rather than rescuing it. Derek Parfit's "Harmless Torturers" case (in *Reasons and Persons*, 1984) has 1,000 torturers each turn a dial by an amount too small for the victim to notice, yet the victim suffers greatly from the combined effect; Parfit treats the inference from "no perceptible difference" to "no morally relevant effect" as one of several mistakes in moral mathematics. Julia Nefsky presses this further in "How You Can Help, Without Making a Difference" (*Philosophical Studies*, 174(11), 2017, pp. 2743–2767), replying directly to Kagan. Her target is an assumption sitting underneath the whole debate: that if an act will not make a difference to an outcome, it cannot play a significant role in bringing that outcome about. She denies it. On her account, "helping" to bring something about is a broader notion than "making a counterfactual difference" to it, and the reason to avoid participating in a collectively harmful practice tracks the former rather than the latter. Her positive proposal distinguishes causal contributions that make genuine progress toward an outcome from those that are merely superfluous to it, a distinction that is demanding to draw but is not the same thing as difference-making.

It is worth being precise about what this does and does not concede, because it is easily conflated with the reply in Sections 5.2 to 5.4. Nefsky is not offering a probabilistic rescue of difference-making of the kind Kagan supplies. She is arguing that the standard should be *replaced* in cases of this shape, not repaired, which is a considerably larger claim, and one that would require rereading much of Section 4's mechanism-design apparatus, since that apparatus takes marginal contribution as its currency throughout. Note also that Nefsky's own car-pushing example, sometimes cited loosely in this connection, functions in her argument as a *contrast* case rather than an illustration: in car-pushing an individual's act is not necessary but could still make a difference, whereas in the collective harm cases that concern her it cannot make any difference at all, and reasoning that succeeds for the former fails for the latter.

The threshold illustration used in Section 5.3 above, of a fixed number of contributors needed to move a load, is the present author's device for making the ex ante and ex post distinction vivid. It should not be attributed to Nefsky, whose argument does not turn on that distinction.

### 5.6 Non-consequentialist routes: universalizability and integrity

Two further replies do not need a causal-efficacy claim at all. Kant's test of universalizability asks whether the maxim "free-ride, since my individual defection is undetectable" could be willed as a universal law; since a world in which everyone reasoned this way would collapse the outcome the free-rider still wants, the maxim is self-undermining independent of what any one instance of it actually causes. Alastair Norcross sharpens the underlying intuition with a thought experiment in "Puppies, Pigs, and People: Eating Meat and Marginal Cases" (*Philosophical Perspectives*, 18(1), 2004, pp. 229–245): Fred tortures a puppy to extract a chemical that improves the taste of his chocolate, in a world already so oversupplied with the chemical that Fred's particular puppy makes no difference to anyone else's future enjoyment. Most people continue to judge Fred a wrongdoer even once his causal irrelevance is stipulated, which suggests the "no difference" defence was never doing the work our first-pass moral judgement of these cases actually depends on. What is doing the work instead is closer to Williams's integrity argument and Wolf's *Moral Saints* (Section 2): "be the change you want to see" is most defensible not as a claim about suffering prevented, but as a claim about what kind of participant in a harmful practice one is willing to be — an integrity-based reason, immune to the inconsequentialism objection precisely because it was never a causal claim in the first place, though correspondingly more modest and agent-relative than the rhetoric of "every little helps" usually implies.

### 5.7 The parallel-disposition unification

The integrity and universalizability replies turn out, on inspection, to be instances of the same structural move that rescued the expected-value reply in Sections 5.2–5.4 — evaluating something at the level of a parallel aggregate rather than a serial chain, only now applied to a disposition rather than to a causal outcome. "A world with lots of good people is better" is not a claim about what one instance of goodness causes; it is a claim about the value of a disposition being widely — that is, parallel-ly — held. This is the explicit architecture of R. M. Hare's two-level utilitarianism (*Moral Thinking: Its Levels, Method, and Point*, Clarendon Press, 1981): at the "critical" level, one asks which dispositions, generally adopted, would produce the best outcomes; at the everyday "intuitive" level, one simply acts on the resulting disposition without recalculating its marginal efficacy case by case. It is also the deeper logic of Railton's sophisticated consequentialism (Section 2.4): the disposition, not the individual act, is what is selected for its consequences, and it is selected by asking what a population of agents with that disposition would produce — a parallel question — not by asking what any single instance of the disposition causes on its own. Parfit's distinction, also in *Reasons and Persons*, between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure states the point most starkly: a consequentialist can coherently want agents *not* to run act-by-act efficacy calculations as their decision procedure, and instead to hold reliable virtues, because a population of reliable-virtue-holders outperforms a population of case-by-case calculators, even by the consequentialist's own criterion. The technical name for a theory that recommends not using itself directly in deliberation is self-effacing consequentialism, and it resolves the apparent tension between Sections 5.2–5.4 and 5.5–5.6: both routes work because both, correctly understood, are parallel-structure arguments — one about the causal structure of the outcome, the other about the justificatory structure of adopting a trait — and both are obscured by the same mistaken serial-chain intuition that makes "my one act can't matter" feel self-evidently true.

### 5.8 Relocating rather than abandoning the causal claim

A further, more modest reply keeps a genuinely causal justification but relocates it. Cristina Bicchieri's *Norms in the Wild* (Oxford University Press, 2016) argues that visible deviation from a prevailing social norm is often the actual mechanism by which other people's beliefs about what is normal, and achievable, shift — a precondition for the kind of coordinated tipping that Rousseau's stag hunt (Section 4.4) and Kagan's own threshold model depend on. On this reading, "be the change you want to see" is a second-order causal claim: not that one meal or one journey changes the outcome directly, but that visibly and consistently acting a certain way raises the probability that enough other people follow that the relevant threshold is eventually crossed — which folds back into the expected-value logic of Sections 5.2–5.4 at one remove, and connects most directly to the mechanism-design material already developed in Sections 4.7 and 4.8. Elizabeth Cripps, in *Climate Change and the Moral Agent: Individual Duties in an Interdependent World* (Oxford University Press, 2013), develops a book-length synthesis for climate specifically, combining a fair-share account of individual duty (echoing Murphy's fair-share view in Section 4.3) with a duty to actively promote the collective mechanisms that would solve the problem, rather than grounding individual duty on direct causal efficacy at all.

### 5.9 Synthesis

None of these routes is individually decisive, but together they explain why the inconsequentialism argument, though logically valid on its own narrow premise, does not settle the practical question. The expected-value reply (5.2–5.4) shows the premise itself — that the individual's actual contribution is zero and nothing further can be said — is usually the wrong model of a genuinely parallel causal structure, whether gated (Kagan) or continuous (Broome), and the voting case shows the resulting expected value need not be trivial even at very large scale. The difference-making challenge (5.5) shows that even where the premise holds exactly, the inference from "no individual difference" to "no reason to act" may itself rest on a mistaken picture of what makes an act part of a harmful or beneficial process. The non-consequentialist and disposition-based routes (5.6–5.7) show that a great deal of the intuitive force behind "be the change you want to see" was never a causal-efficacy claim to begin with, but a parallel-structure claim about character or universalizable policy that the inconsequentialism argument was never positioned to touch. And the norm-signalling route (5.8) shows that even a strictly causal defence can survive by relocating from the direct outcome to the beliefs and behaviour of others. What unifies all four is the diagnosis in Section 5.3: the inconsequentialism argument borrows its force from modelling individual action as one fragile link in a long, decaying chain, when the structures actually at stake — in voting, consumption, emissions, and character alike — are parallel ones, in which redundancy strengthens rather than weakens the case for participating.

One further point ties this section back to Section 4. Causal texture — serial or parallel — is not an intrinsic property of a phenomenon; it is a function of how finely the population of contributors is partitioned. The same additive process that is parallel and redundant at the grain of billions of individual queries, voters, or purchasers becomes serial, or close to it, at the grain of a handful of large institutional actors, because coarsening the partition shrinks the number of units while inflating each remaining unit's share of the total, and pivotality tracks share, not headcount. This is not merely intuitive: voting-power theory measures it directly. Lloyd Shapley and Martin Shubik's power index ("A Method for Evaluating the Distribution of Power in a Committee System," *American Political Science Review*, 48(3), 1954, pp. 787–792) defines an actor's power as exactly the ex ante probability of being the vote that converts a losing coalition into a winning one, and John Banzhaf's "Weighted Voting Doesn't Work: A Mathematical Analysis" (*Rutgers Law Review*, 19, 1965, pp. 317–343) showed empirically that grouping voters into blocs of different sizes redistributes this pivotal power in ways that can be wildly disproportionate to raw vote share — in some weighted voting bodies a bloc can hold real votes and precisely zero power to ever be decisive. This resolves what could otherwise look like a tension between Section 4 and Section 5: a state's decision to join or defect from a climate club (Section 4.8) is correctly treated as a near-necessary, serial-feeling choice, because there are only a handful of states large enough to matter, while an individual voter's preference within that same state is correctly treated as parallel and non-pivotal (Sections 5.2–5.4), because there are millions of them. Both descriptions are true, at their own grain, simultaneously — the same underlying aggregation, viewed at two different resolutions.

## 6. How Should We Think About Moral Inconsistency Itself?

### 6.1 Plural, sometimes-competing duties as the normal condition

W. D. Ross's *The Right and the Good* (Clarendon Press, Oxford, 1930) offers a framework for expecting exactly this kind of tension without treating it as a crisis. Ross argues that morality consists of several distinct *prima facie* duties — including fidelity, gratitude, justice, and beneficence — none of which is reducible to the others, and which can conflict in particular situations. On a Rossian view, a duty of special concern for one's family (a kind of fidelity) and a duty of justice/beneficence toward strangers can both be genuine, simultaneously binding duties that sometimes pull in different directions; the task in any given case is to judge which duty is weightier there, not to find a single master principle from which all others are derived without remainder. This directly supports the diagnosis reached in Sections 2–3: it is not obviously a mistake to hold both an equality-based and a partiality-based commitment at once.

### 6.2 Reflective equilibrium

John Rawls' method in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), formalized by Norman Daniels in "Wide Reflective Equilibrium and Theory Acceptance in Ethics" (*Journal of Philosophy*, 76(5), 1979, pp. 256–282), treats moral theorizing as an iterative process of adjusting general principles and particular judgements against each other until they cohere — revising a principle when it produces an intuitively unacceptable verdict, or revising a strongly-held intuition when a well-supported principle tells against it. On this view, noticing an apparent inconsistency (as the reader has done) is not itself a failure; it is the *starting point* of exactly the process by which moral views are properly refined. The cases above are, in effect, worked examples of reflective equilibrium in action: in each, closer analysis of the *reasons* behind the intuitions (rather than simply the intuitions' surface content) reveals distinctions — equal worth vs. equal treatment, relationship goods vs. wealth transfer, compulsory fair share vs. voluntary sacrifice, difference-making vs. parallel participation — that the initial, loosely-stated principles were eliding.

### 6.3 Psychological accounts of maintaining inconsistent beliefs

It is also worth acknowledging the purely psychological dimension. Leon Festinger's *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance* (Stanford University Press, 1957) describes how people generally respond to inconsistency between belief and behaviour not by changing the behaviour, but by adjusting belief, attention, or justification to reduce the discomfort — which is a caution against assuming that any post-hoc rationalization of an inconsistency (including some of the theoretical distinctions surveyed above) is *automatically* sound just because it relieves the discomfort of noticing the tension. Joshua Greene's *Moral Tribes: Emotion, Reason, and the Gap Between Us and Them* (Penguin/Atlantic Books, 2013) offers a complementary dual-process account: fast, automatic, emotionally-loaded moral intuitions (tuned, evolutionarily, for small-group, kin-based cooperation — see Hamilton 1964 above) often diverge from the slower, effortful, more impartial reasoning humans are also capable of. On Greene's account, the pull toward partiality and the pull toward impartial fairness are quite literally generated by different cognitive systems built for different purposes, which is one further explanation of why the tension is felt so persistently even after it has been intellectually addressed.

### 6.4 Overall assessment

Putting the parts of this paper together: the partiality/equality tension (Section 2) and the inheritance/parental-provision tension (Section 3) both dissolve substantially once "equality" and "providing for children" are disaggregated into the more precise concepts that the philosophical literature independently uses — equal standing versus equal treatment; relationship goods versus wealth transfer. The taxation/donation tension (Section 4) dissolves *less completely*: there is a solid institutional argument for the asymmetry, but a serious counter-tradition (Cohen, and the strict Singer view) keeps part of the original discomfort alive as a legitimate, still-open philosophical question. Section 5's inquiry into inconsequentialism cuts across all three: it shows that even where a mechanism-design fix is unavailable and an individual's actual causal contribution truly is negligible, several independent lines of argument still supply a reason to act — which means the absence of a Section 4-style institutional solution does not, by itself, let anyone off the hook. None of this is evidence of a personal failing. It is, if anything, evidence that the reader's untutored intuitions have been tracking real and difficult distinctions that professional moral and political philosophy has spent over a century working out — not always to agreement.

## 7. Conclusion

The three inconsistencies with which this paper began are not merely instances of applying a principle to everyone else and quietly excusing oneself. Each rests on a substantive theoretical question that remains live in contemporary philosophy: how much latitude impartial morality grants for partial relationships (Williams, Wolf, Railton, Scheffler); where the line falls between legitimate parental provision and illegitimate transmission of unearned advantage (Rawls, Fishkin, Nozick, Mill, Swift and Brighouse, Piketty); and whether support for coercive collective redistribution discharges one's personal duty of beneficence, or merely relocates the question (Murphy and Nagel, Olson, Hardin, Sen, and Hirschman on why unilateral, uncoordinated action differs from binding collective commitment, versus Cohen and Singer, who insist personal conduct still matters). A fourth, more fundamental question emerged from pressing on the third: whether an individual's action can be justified at all once its actual causal contribution is negligible — and the serial/parallel distinction developed in Section 5 suggests that the "no difference" objection borrows most of its force from a model of causal relevance that quietly, and often wrongly, treats redundant, many-handed action as if it were a single fragile chain. Read against this literature, moral consistency turns out to be less a matter of applying one flat rule everywhere, and more a matter of being able to say *why* a given distinction is doing legitimate work — which, on inspection, the reader's own intuitions largely already can.
