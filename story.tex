\partheading{I}{The Commission}
\noindent They rang him into the county at Brentwood, as they always did, and by the time the coaches came up the hill into the assize town the bells had been going the better part of an hour, and the javelin-men rode ahead with their points up in the March light, and the trumpets sounded at the bridge, and Sir Nathaniel Verey, one of Her Majesty's justices of the Queen's Bench, sat in the sheriff's coach with his robes in a press behind him and watched the county turn out to see the law arrive, as it had turned out every spring and summer of his eleven years upon the circuit; and reflected that no man who had once seen the entrance of the judges could doubt that the law was, among its other trades, a spectacle, and kept its terms like a playhouse.

The sheriff talked of the roads, and of the winter, which had been past anything in his time or his father's; of cattle dead in the byre, and birds found frozen out of the air, and the mills stopped six weeks together with the rivers gone to stone. Half the talk in the county was still of the great cold. The other half, the sheriff said, arranging his gloves, his lordship would find in the calendar.

At the lodging Harcourt saw the boxes up, and hung the weather-glass by the window with a spirit level and a gravity that made the landlady stare, and Verey dined early and afterwards sat with the calendar and the depositions, as his custom was, in the room they had given him over the square.

The packet from the committing justice, Sir Giles Fanshawe, was thicker than the rest and tied with better string, which told him something of what the county thought of it before he had read a word. He read for two hours. Margery Ashe, of Salt Harrow, spinster, committed upon the statute of the first year of King James for that she did wickedly and feloniously use, practise, and exercise witchcraft upon Tobias Crane, corn-factor, whereby the said Tobias Crane died. The depositions in their stiff clerk's hand: Sarah Hart, widow, that on the Thursday before Michaelmas she did see and hear the prisoner at the door of Tobias Crane; Ellen Tyce, to the like effect; Nathan and Amos Fisk, brothers, carters, to the like effect; and the words themselves, written out fair upon a quarter-sheet and exhibited, which Verey read three times, and then once aloud, alone in the room, to hear what they were.

Let all doors be shut against Tobias Crane; let no man grind for him, nor house him, nor lend to him; let him want bread among plenty, and warmth in the frost, till he pay what he owes.

Then the long tail of the matter. That from Michaelmas the man's trade sickened; the mill closed to him, and the inn, and his paper called in, and his name struck from the coal charity at Christmas, in the hardest year for coal that any warden could remember. And in January, upon the Ardleigh road, a carrier going in before light had found him under the hedge with his boots on, frozen, three miles from his own door, and not a door in the three miles that would have opened to him. The coroner's inquisition: died by the visitation of God, that is to say, of the extremity of the cold. And at the end, in Fanshawe's own hand, the return of the searches: the prisoner's house turned over by the constable, and her person searched by two sworn women of the parish, and nothing found; no image of wax or clay, no bones, no writing save her book of trade; which book Sir Giles had thought fit to send up with the papers, and there it lay at the bottom of the packet, a plain shopkeeper's day-book with a marbled cover, such as Verey's own father had kept forty of.

His father had been a Levant merchant, and had bred him to accounts before ever he was bred to the law, and had held, as an article of faith stronger than most of his religion, that a man's books do not lie to him unless he has first asked them to. Verey left the day-book where it lay. It was for the court, not the lodging. But he sat a while looking at the cover, and then rose and went to the window, where the glass hung new-levelled in the light of the one candle, and tapped it, and watched the quicksilver tremble and settle, high, promising fair.

It foretells, he thought, and does not make. His father had bought him the first one when he was nine years old, in the Amsterdam trade, and had said something of the same kind: the glass, Nat, is the only honest servant you will ever keep, for it will tell you what is coming and take no credit when it comes. He had trusted the instrument all his life on those terms. He went to his bed without pressing the thought further, for it had as yet no shape that would bear pressing: only a small unease, come up out of the depositions to stand somewhere behind him, that in the matter of Margery Ashe the county kept an instrument it consulted in the same spirit; and that nobody in Essex had ever thought to ask whether the quicksilver made the weather.

\scenebreak
\noindent The sermon was preached the next morning by the sheriff's chaplain, a smooth young man with an eye on better livings, upon a text out of the hundred and forty-seventh Psalm: He casteth forth his ice like morsels: who can stand before his cold? A well-judged text, Verey allowed, for a county with its dead scarcely thawed, and the young man made a fair thing of it. God, he said, does not govern the world by prodigies, but by the ordinary instruments of it; His frosts come by the north wind, His harvests by the ploughman, His judgments, and here he bowed toward the bench, by the Queen's justices; and it is no diminishment of Providence that its workings can be traced, for the tracing is only the reading of His hand in second causes.

Verey sat under this with his chin sunk in his bands and found it sounder doctrine than the preacher knew. It cut every way at once. If the Almighty might work His will through frost and ploughmen, none of the divines in England could say why the Devil might not work his through millers and innkeepers; and none of them, either, could say why a thing that worked through millers and innkeepers need have any devil in it at all. The whole question of the assize sat inside the sermon like a wasp in a windfall, and the preacher never felt the sting, and pronounced the blessing very handsomely.

Coming out into the churchyard Verey passed among the county at its devotions: the grand jurymen in their good broadcloth, the witnesses up from Salt Harrow in a knot by the porch, a widow in decent black whom Harcourt murmured to be Goodwife Hart, the first name on the depositions. They made their reverences as he passed. Every soul of them, he thought, returning the salutes, believes the woman in the gaol can kill with a word, and has believed it since the cradle; and they will be my jury, and my witnesses, and my county; and what I chiefly know of the matter that they do not is contained in a toy of quicksilver hanging at my window, and in my father's opinion of account-books.

\scenebreak
\noindent He delivered his charge to the grand jury that afternoon, the courtroom half-empty, as it always was for the law and full for the blood. Three and twenty gentlemen of the county, Sir Giles Fanshawe himself at the head of them, attending the bench with the particular gravity of men who expect to be told their duty and have already resolved upon it.

He went through the calendar in order, the coining, the horse-thefts, the infanticide at Coggeshall, keeping his usual pace, and came to the bill against Margery Ashe last, and paused, and chose the plain register.

`Gentlemen, you have also before you a bill upon the statute of witchcraft of the first year of King James, which statute, whatever may be said in the coffee-houses of London, stands unrepealed, and is the law of England, and you are not to repeal it by incredulity in this room. If the evidence laid before you would, being unanswered, warrant a trial, you will find the bill, whatever any of you may privately hold concerning the art it speaks of. But I direct you likewise, and you will carry this with you into your chamber: the statute is not a statute against ill words, nor ill wishes, nor the reputation of power. It is a statute against killing. The bill alleges that Tobias Crane died by the prisoner's witchcraft, and the law hangs no one for words unless the words be shown a means of death; and you are to look, in what is sworn before you, for that means, and not only for the words and the dying, which two things may stand a mile apart with all of Essex between them.

`And I will say one thing more, gentlemen, for it belongs to the conscience of this county in particular. No shire in England has sent more women to the gallows upon this statute than Essex, and the most of them, as our grandfathers lived to own, were poor, old, and friendless creatures whose whole witchcraft was to be a burden to their parish. If the bill is true, find it, and fear nobody. But you will not find it because it is old custom in this county to find it; the custom has too many graves already.'

They filed out. Verey heard other bills while they deliberated, and watched the door of their chamber over the top of a deposition concerning a stolen gelding, and was not made to wait long. The foreman brought the bills in a sheaf, and the clerk read the endorsements, and against the bill for Margery Ashe: billa vera. A true bill. It had taken them, by Verey's watch, less time than the horse.

He had expected nothing else, and told himself so, and made his note. But he sat a moment before calling the next business, looking at the three and twenty gentlemen of the county as they resumed their places, sound men, substantial men, not one of whom would have found a true bill against the north wind; and he understood that the finding was itself a piece of evidence, though of what, and admissible where, he could not yet have said. The county had been asked, in form of law, whether it believed; and the county had answered on its oath; and the trial that was to come would be held inside that answer, as the town was held inside its walls.

\partheading{II}{The Trial}
\noindent The court was full by seven, and the yard fuller, and they brought Margery Ashe to the bar between two turnkeys at eight. Verey took his first proper look at her while the indictment was read: a spare upright woman of fifty or thereabouts, decently dressed, grey under a clean cap, who attended to the Latin and the English of the charge with her head a little on one side, as though checking a bill of goods against delivery.

`How sayest thou, Margery Ashe: art thou guilty of the felony and witchcraft whereof thou standest indicted, or not guilty?'

`Not guilty.' A shop voice, clear to the back wall, used to carrying over a market.

`Culprit, how wilt thou be tried?'

`By God and my country.'

`God send thee a good deliverance.'

The jury was sworn without challenge, which interested Verey; a prisoner fighting for her life may challenge her jurors, and this one watched all twelve to the book, one after another, with her tradesman's tilt of the head, and let every man of them pass. Either she did not know her right, which he doubted, for she seemed a woman who knew to a farthing what was owed her in any transaction, or she had reckoned the county so much of one mind that the choosing of twelve was indifferent, which was, he conceded, sound arithmetic.

Serjeant Corse rose to open, an old man gone stiff in the shoulders, with a voice kept in repair a half-century for exactly such mornings, and laid out the Crown's case as a man lays a pavement, stone against stone, no gaps.

`Gentlemen of the jury. On the Thursday before Michaelmas the prisoner at the bar stood at the door of Tobias Crane and cursed him, before witnesses, in words you will hear proved to the syllable. She asked of God, or of whatever power attends her askings, that every door be shut against him; and gentlemen, every door was shut. That his corn go unground; and it went unground. That no man house him nor lend to him; and no man housed him, and no man lent. That he want bread among plenty and warmth in the frost; and he wanted both, in a county whose barns, God be thanked, stood full all winter. And that this estate continue till he pay what he owed; and he never paid, gentlemen, and it never ceased, and in January the county took him up frozen under a hedge on the Ardleigh road, three miles from the door she cursed him at. She asked; it was given; he is dead. You will be told that the winter was hard, and that it killed men no one had cursed. So it did, gentlemen; it killed them where no bread or fire could reach them. Tobias Crane died three miles from both, in a parish that wanted for neither, with not a door in it he could darken. The frost was general; the famine was his alone.

`Nor is this the law's first acquaintance with such causes. In the late King's time, at Bury St Edmunds, my Lord Chief Baron Hale, than whom this nation has produced no greater ornament of her law, left such a cause to a jury of Suffolk and the jury found the felony, and the law was satisfied. The art is dark; the proof of it, my lord Hale said, is as in other cases, by the fruits. You will have the fruits proved before you, gentlemen: the asking, and the granting, and the corpse. What went between is the prisoner's secret, and the Crown does not undertake to light a candle in another world; it undertakes to show you that in this one, what that woman said, came to pass, entire, in order, and upon no other man.'

He sat. The gallery let out its breath in a long rustle of assent, and Verey, making his note, observed once more the thing he had observed at the grand jury: the county's conviction ran ahead of its evidence like a dog before a horse, and had been at the ditch and back before the first witness was sworn.

\scenebreak
\noindent The morning went to the cursing itself, and the Crown had it proved past any cavil. Goodwife Hart, decent and terrified, had stood not six feet off and heard every word; Ellen Tyce the same from her own doorway; the Fisk brothers, carters, had stopped their team to listen, and Amos Fisk, invited by the Serjeant to repeat what he had heard, delivered the curse from memory with a relish that drew a rebuke from the bench, and delivered it word-perfect, which Verey noted with more interest than the rebuke. A market-cross recitation, five months after one hearing. The words had been kept bright by many tellings.

The afternoon went to the chain, and the chain, under Verey's questions, began its widening.

Pead the miller, a slab of a man, swore that from Michaelmas he would not take Crane's corn. The Serjeant sat down content. Verey leaned forward.

`Tell the court why not.'

`Why, the man was cried, my lord.'

`Was that the whole of your reason? What was the state of his account with you at Michaelmas?'

A silence, in which the miller's face performed the labour of a man deciding between his oath and his standing. `He was two seasons behind, my lord. Rising three.'

`Had you ground for him at Lady Day, before ever he was cried, what security had you looked for?'

`Ready money, my lord,' said Pead, unhappily, and the first small draught of doubt went through the courtroom, felt rather than heard.

Pettit the innkeeper, examined, had barred Crane his house from Michaelmas. Why? The man was cried. Any other cause? Well: there had been the business at harvest, Crane drunk and a settle broken and the reckoning disputed before the whole parlour; his wife had wanted the man barred then, and he had half done it. Rundle the lender, a dry, exact person who gave his answers like coin told out on a counter, had called in Crane's paper the first week of October. Because the man was cried? Rundle looked pained. He had called in a great deal of paper that autumn, my lord; the harvest had been poor and the winter promised worse, and money was dear; he could, if the court wished, name six other men whose paper he had called the same fortnight, none of them cursed by anybody. The Serjeant rose at that, quickly, and drew from him that of those six, five had found accommodation elsewhere in the county and the sixth had a brother in Norwich; only Crane had found every purse in Essex shut at once; and Rundle allowed it, and Corse sat down having mended his stone. But the stone had been lifted, and the whole court had seen the shape of the ground under it.

Last, the carrier who had found the body, and the coroner's clerk with the inquisition, and the plain arithmetic of that January: the coldest night in the memory of England, and half the county burying someone before Candlemas, cursed and uncursed alike.

Verey kept his notes in the margin of the calendar in his own abbreviation, and toward the end of the afternoon caught himself at a reckoning of a kind he had no name for. Say the mill was shut to Crane nine parts in ten for the cry, and one part for the debt; say the inn the same, and the paper, and the charity, and the roads and the frost at the end of all; what then was left of the Serjeant's pavement, laid stone against stone, each stone sound, and every joint of it giving a little? He looked at his row of nines and could not make them into anything a court could use; there was no verdict of guilty in nine parts. But the figures sat on the page and would not be nothing, either; and he drew a line under them, and wrote against the line, in his own shorthand, the thought that had been forming all afternoon. The common saying had it that a chain is no stronger than its weakest link. His figures said a blacker thing: that a chain is weaker than any link in it, the soundest included, for every joint pays its toll; and then, because the thought had a familiar taste and he could not place the kitchen it came from, he sat a moment chasing it, and the court waited on him, and he let it go and called the next witness.

\scenebreak
\noindent Toward evening the Serjeant played his best card. The constable of Salt Harrow produced the day-book, taken up at the search, marbled covers, a tradesman's book; and Corse, receiving it as a priest receives the vessel, carried it to the jury-box rail and read.

`Of Goodman Ellery, for a blessing upon his cattle at the murrain, one shilling. Done. Of Widow Kett, for the finding of two silver spoons, sixpence. Found, and the girl dismissed. Of Mr Tanner of Bergholt, for a crying against one Judd that had ploughed his headland, three shillings. Done, and the headland restored before Whitsun. Of Goody Fisher, for her daughter's ague, sixpence. Done.' He turned the leaves slowly, and the entries fell on the court like slow strokes of a bell. `Of Mr Sewell, for a crying against the thatcher Gosnold in the matter of his roof, four shillings. Done; the thatcher removed into Suffolk. Of Mr Dunthorne, maltster, for a crying against Tobias Crane, corn-factor, in the matter of his debt, six shillings. Cried at his door the Thursday, before witnesses.' He paused, and looked up at the jury over the rim of the book, and read the last of it in the voice he had been saving all day. `And after, in another ink: done.'

`Seventeen years, gentlemen,' said Corse, closing the covers with a sound that carried. `Two hundred and eleven workings, by my clerk's count, and against every one of them, this same word. There is not a tradesman on this circuit, there is not a physician in London, gentlemen, who could show you such a book. You are asked to believe there is nothing in her art. Her customers were of another opinion, and they paid in silver, seventeen years together, and the book says they had their money's worth every man. By the fruits, gentlemen. There are the fruits, done up in marbled covers.'

He laid the book upon the exhibits table with the small deliberate noise of a trump going down, and the court was silent, and the silence was of the Crown's kind.

Verey called for the book to be brought up to the bench, and turned its leaves while the shadows got longer, twice through, and then a third time, no longer reading the entries but the ruling of the pages, the columns, the manner of the keeping; and the merchant's son in him, dead these forty years, came quietly back from wherever such men wait, and put a dry finger on the page, and asked the one question of it that his father would have asked of any book in the Levant trade before he ventured sixpence on the strength of it.

Verey looked up. `In whose hand is this book kept? It is not the prisoner's; hers is on the curse-paper, and it is a better hand than this.'

The constable consulted his memory. `The servant girl's, my lord. Susanna Reed. She is below among the witnesses, being summoned by the Crown and not yet called.'

`The Crown may call her in the morning or not, as my brother pleases,' said Verey. `The court will hear her in any case. Let her be ready at the sitting.' And he adjourned the court with the book still open on the cushion before him, at no particular page, since every page was the same page; and what the pages said, and what no one in seventeen years had thought to remark, was that in all that fair-ruled shopkeeping there was no column, and had never been a column, for the workings that failed.

\scenebreak
\noindent The girl came to the book like a thing driven, and took the oath in a voice Verey had to lean forward to catch. Susanna Reed, of Salt Harrow, servant to the prisoner these four years; sixteen at Lady Day. She kept her eyes on the rail of the box, and her hands worked in her apron as if she were wringing it dry.

Verey had not intended much by calling her. The day-book lay open before him on the cushion, and it wanted only its keeper to settle its keeping: whether the entries were made at the day or afterwards, at whose word, in whose hand. It was the sort of question he would have put to a clothier's apprentice in a cause of debt, and he put it in the same voice.

`This book is yours, child?'

`The hand is mine, my lord. The book is my mistress's.'

`And the entries. Are they made upon the day?'

`Of an evening, my lord. My mistress tells me the day's business at supper, and I set it down, and she looks it over after, for she reads very well.' The girl said this last with a small, absurd pride, as one defending a mistress's accomplishments to strangers, and a ripple of amusement went along the gallery. Verey let it pass. He turned the leaves slowly, so that the jury might see him do it.

`Here is an entry of Michaelmas last,' he said. `Read it to the court.'

She read without stumbling, which told him she had read it many times. `Of Mr Dunthorne, maltster, for a crying against Tobias Crane, corn-factor, in the matter of his debt, six shillings. Cried at his door the Thursday before witnesses.' She paused, and her thumb moved on the rail. `And after, in another ink: done.'

`In whose hand is that last word?'

`Mine, my lord.'

`Set down at whose bidding?'

`My mistress's.'

`And when?'

The girl looked, for the first time, toward the prisoner at the bar, and Margery Ashe looked back at her with a stillness that Verey could not read, neither warning nor absolution, only attention. `In February, my lord. When the news came up from the road.'

There was no sound in the court then but the shifting of the Serjeant in his place, a small, satisfied settling. Verey understood the settling: the girl had just sworn the death into her mistress's own book of account. He made his note, and he had, at that moment, every intention of releasing her. It was the book itself that stopped him. He had been through it twice at the bench, and the merchant's son in him, dead these forty years, had been through it a third time unbidden, and had come back troubled by a thing no one had yet said aloud.

`Child,' he said, `here are seventeen years of your mistress's business, and better than two hundred workings, and against every one of them, soon or late, this same word, done. In all this book I find no entry marked otherwise. Is there another book, for the failures?'

`There are no failures, my lord.'

She said it as she had said the hand is mine, a plain fact of the shop, and the gallery stirred, and Serjeant Corse permitted himself to look at the jury. Verey was aware that he had just done the Crown's work for it, and was about to leave the matter there, and did not, for the same reason a man who has found one false figure in a ledger cannot help but sum the column entire.

`Very well. Then tell the court how a working is done, that it should never fail. Take the crying against Crane. Your mistress took Mr Dunthorne's six shillings. What then? What is done in the shop?'

`The paper is written, my lord.'

Verey had the paper before him, among the exhibits, a quarter-sheet in a fair round hand, and he read it to the court, because it seemed to him the jury ought to hear the instrument they were asked to call a means of death. `Let all doors be shut against Tobias Crane; let no man grind for him, nor house him, nor lend to him; let him want bread among plenty, and warmth in the frost, till he pay what he owes.' He laid it down. `This paper. And then?'

`Then my mistress goes to the man's door, and speaks the words before witnesses, and comes home.'

`And then? What working follows? What is done with fire, or wax, or image? The constable's men searched your mistress's house, and the searchers her person, and found nothing of that kind. Where is it done?'

`Nothing is done, my lord.' The girl's brow creased, as if the question itself were strange to her, a thing asked from a country where they did these matters differently. `There is nothing of that kind in our shop, nor ever was. My mistress speaks the words at the door and comes home and has her dinner.'

The gallery laughed, one loud crack of it, and the crier called for silence. Verey did not smile. Something had shifted in his understanding, quietly, the way ice shifts on a pond, from below.

`Attend to me, child. You have sworn there are no failures. And you now swear that after the speaking, nothing further is done. Then tell the court how the thing is accomplished. By what means does the curse proceed from the door to the man?'

The girl was silent for a moment, and in her silence Verey observed that she was not searching for an answer but for the words of it, as a workman asked suddenly to describe an operation his hands have done ten thousand times.

`Why, it is cried before witnesses, my lord,' she said at last. `That it be known. Goodwife Hart was by, and Ellen Tyce, and the two Fisk brothers, and it was over the street by dinner and at the mill by evening, and cried at the market cross the Saturday, and there is not a soul in Salt Harrow nor two parishes round but had it within the week. That is why it is done at the door, and not privately. A curse spoken privately is', and she sought the word, and found a shopkeeper's word, `wasted.'

`And then?'

`And then it is known, my lord.'

`And what follows from its being known?'

`What always follows.' She looked at him with the first touch of impatience, the impatience of the literal-minded for the slow. `The mill will not take his corn. Master Pettit will not draw him beer. Rundle the lender calls in his paper and will write him no more. The carters will not carry for him, and the wardens strike him from the coal charity, and no man at market will stand next his standing. Not', and here she grew careful, and Verey heard in her care the whole of it coming, the way a man hears a cart on a bridge before he sees it, `not that any of them is bid to, my lord. No one is bid. My mistress speaks to no one of it after. But each man knows the man is cursed, and no man will hazard his own luck, nor his custom, nor his good name, on a marked man. Each keeps his own door, for his own reasons. And so it comes to pass of itself, and in the book I write done.'

Serjeant Corse was rising; Verey put out two fingers from the cushion, no more, and the Serjeant sat.

`Let me have this plainly,' Verey said, and his own voice sounded strange to him, too even, the voice he kept for the reading of verdicts. `You are telling this court that after the speaking at the door, your mistress works nothing, sends nothing, and touches nothing; and that what kills is that the parish, every soul of it severally, knowing the man marked, shuts against him of its own accord.'

`Yes, my lord.' And then, because she was sixteen, and honest past all rescue, and had spent four years learning her trade as another girl learns lace, she said the sentence that Verey would carry out of that courtroom and through the summer circuit and, he already suspected, to the end of his service: `The curse is the telling of it, my lord. There is nothing else in it at all.'

The court had grown quiet by degrees while she spoke, and now the quiet completed itself, and became a different kind of quiet. Verey looked out over it. The four witnesses to the cursing sat in the second row, Goodwife Hart with her hands folded as if at a sermon. Behind them the miller, who would not grind; and Pettit, who would not draw; and Rundle, who had called in the paper, sat each in his place with the particular stillness of men listening to an inventory and hearing, item by item, their own goods called. The gallery had come to see a witch tried. It had just heard itself described, severally and by name, as the instrument; and no one laughed now, and no one moved, and from the bar Margery Ashe watched her servant with an expression that Verey finally recognised, and wished he had not. It was the look of a tradesman hearing the secret of the shop given away for nothing: not fear, and not anger, but a kind of mourning.

The frost, Verey thought, killed him no more than the paper did. The frost only kept the doors shut faster.

He became aware that the court was waiting on him, and that he had been silent too long, and that the Serjeant was on his feet again with the light of recovery in his face, having seen, quicker than the gallery, that what the girl had given away cut with both edges. Verey inclined his head a fraction, and gave him leave, and set his pen down beside the open book, next the column with one word in it.

\scenebreak
\noindent Corse, on his feet already and with the court's leave in his hand, took a moment before spending it, as an old man will who means the pause itself to be part of the speech.

`My lord, I am obliged to the court, and to the honesty of this child, more than my learned friends below the bar may yet perceive. For what has she sworn? She has sworn to the manner of the killing. The Crown alleged a dark art and confessed it could not light the darkness; the girl has lit it. This woman's art, it now appears, is to take up a whole parish as a man takes up a piece from the chimney-corner, that stands harmless there year in and year out; and to point it; and to discharge it at the breast of one man. Gentlemen, if I load and level a musket and it kills, it is idle in me to say the powder did it, and the ball did it, and I did nothing but crook my finger. The parish was her powder and ball. She knew its temper, none better, for she had spent seventeen years keeping it primed, at sixpence and a shilling a time; she knew to a certainty what must follow her crying at that door, as surely as any musketeer knows what follows the flash; and what followed was death, foreseen, purchased, and delivered. My lord, the means is proved out of the mouth of her own servant, and it is the surer for having no devil in it. I never yet hanged the Devil. I have hanged them that pulled triggers.'

It was formidably done, and Verey saw it go home along the jury-box, and thought: there is the true shape of the Crown's case at last, and it took an old man half a morning and a servant girl to find it, and it is better law than the bill it stands on. He looked to the bar.

`Mr Fenn. The court will hear the prisoner's counsel upon the matter of law.'

Fenn was up like a partridge. A young man, Middle Temple, sent down by whom and paid by whom Verey had chosen not to inquire; he had sat two days taking notes with his ears red, and this was his one lawful entrance, and he made it count.

`My lord. The indictment is upon the statute, and the statute requires that the death be by witchcraft, invocation, charm, or sorcery. Upon the evidence there is no witchcraft; there is a speaking of words, and there is the several conduct of some hundreds of the Queen's subjects thereafter, every act of which, taken by itself, is lawful. My learned friend the Serjeant says musket. My lord, a musket has no will. Powder does not choose to take the spark; it is the nature of powder, and the law therefore lays all upon the finger. But Pead the miller is not powder. Pead the miller is a man, who might have ground, and chose not to, upon his own reasons, in his own right, answerable to his own conscience; and so the innkeeper, and so the lender, and so every shut door in Salt Harrow. The prisoner's words travelled no further than the ears of four witnesses. Everything after was done by choosing minds she did not govern and could not compel. My lord, if a man may hang because free men, hearing him, chose severally to do lawful things, then every scold in England stands in peril of the rope, and so does every preacher of a hard sermon that empties a sinner's pew. The statute wants a means, and a means in law must be the doer's own; and the Crown's means, upon its own evidence, is the county of Essex; and the county of Essex is not indicted.'

`The book, Mr Fenn,' said Verey, because it was the point the jury would stumble at, and better stumbled at now, in argument, than in their chamber. `Two hundred and eleven workings, and done against every one. Does the county of Essex account for the book?'

`My lord, the book proves nothing, and I will say why. It is kept, we now know, upon this principle: what comes to pass is entered, and what fails is not entered, for there is no place in its ruling for failure at all. My lord, there was an archer once who was held the surest shot in his hundred, for that every arrow of his stood in a clout; and so it did; for his manner was to loose first, and paint the clout about the arrow after. The book is that archer's book of his shooting. Two hundred and eleven arrows, my lord, and a pot of paint.'

The gallery laughed, and it was the first laughter of the trial that was on the prisoner's side of the room, and Verey let it run a moment, for the picture would keep better in twelve memories than an hour of law. Then Corse rose again, and did not wait for silence but took it.

`A pot of paint. Very good. I will grant my young friend his archer, my lord; I will grant that in the first years of that book, some clouts were painted, the sixpenny agues, the found spoons, the cattle that would have mended anyway. What of it? For seventeen years that book has been read aloud in every alehouse in three parishes. The county learned it as it learns a catechism: what she cries, comes. And, gentlemen,' he turned to the box, and his voice came down from oratory to something older and plainer, `because the county believes it, the county performs it. You have heard how, from the child. The doors shut of themselves. The painted clouts, my lord, have taught every arrow since to fly true. Ask the prisoner, ask her upon her life, whether one entry of the last seven years in that book is false; whether one man cried in that book did not suffer what the book says. She dare not deny it, for the parish is in this room that watched every one of them come to pass.'

Verey looked at the bar, and the court looked with him.

`The prisoner may answer that if she chooses. Is any entry of these late years false?'

Margery Ashe considered the question as she seemed to consider everything, as an item on an invoice, to be checked against goods received, and answered in the shop voice, without defiance and without fear, the four words that Verey knew, even as he heard them, the shorthand men were taking down for the bookstalls of London:

`Not one, my lord.'

\scenebreak
\noindent The law allowed her no counsel to speak to the facts, and so, the evidence being closed, the court turned to her, and Verey told her, in the words he used to every prisoner, that now was her time, and she should say what she would in her own defence, and the jury would hear her.

She stood at the bar with her hands folded at her waist, and spoke unsworn, without paper, in the voice that carried; and if she had spent her two nights in the gaol composing it, Verey thought, then she had spent them as a lawyer spends his, for she went to the seat of the matter like a woman who had drawn the indictment herself.

`My lords, and gentlemen of the jury. I shall not waste the court's candle denying what four honest witnesses have sworn. I said those words at that man's door, and took Mr Dunthorne's six shillings for the saying, and I am sorry the man is dead, and I did not kill him.

`Gentlemen, you have heard my girl tell you the secret of my trade, which I had sooner she had not, for it was my living; but since it is out, I will deal with you as plainly as she did. I sell what every trade in this kingdom sells, and no court has ever hanged them for it: I sell the customer his own opinion, made to work for him. The physician writes his Latin, and the sick man mends; did the Latin mend him? The parson prays over the dry field, and the rain comes; gentlemen, you pay them both, year in, year out, upon no better proof of means than stands against me in that book, and you would think it madness to indict either when a patient dies or a harvest fails. It is only my trade that must show its engine or hang.

`And I will say this of intent, for I heard the learned Serjeant's musket. When Mr Dunthorne came to my shop he did not buy a death. He bought his four pounds back; you have heard the words; till he pay what he owes; that is the whole compass of it, and there was a door out of that curse standing open day and night, and the key of it was four pounds, and half the men in this room have owed more and slept sound. That Tobias Crane would sooner freeze than pay, or sooner freeze than ask kindness of them he had cheated, was in the man, gentlemen, and not in my words. I have cried many men. The book is there; the court has read it. They paid, or they made terms, or they removed and began elsewhere, and the matter ended; the thatcher Gosnold is thriving this day in Suffolk. No man ever died of my crying before, and no man needed to die of this one, at any hour of any day, for four pounds.

`And last, my lord, since my neck is in it, I will ask the court one question, and then I have done.' She turned from the jury to the bench, and Verey, meeting her look, knew what was coming the instant before it came, as a man knows the lightning by the stillness. `When your lordship condemns a man at this bar, and he is taken out, and thereafter no master in the county will take his sons to service, nor any mill grind his widow's corn, and his family are broken and gone to the parish within the twelvemonth, all of which your lordship has seen follow your sentences as surely as ever ruin followed my cryings; what did that, my lord? The rope? Or the telling?'

The court came up out of its benches in a body of sound, and the crier was on his feet, and Serjeant Corse was on his, and Verey with two words, `Sit down,' restored a silence in which his own voice sounded to him like a stranger's.

`The question is improper, and the jury will disregard it. Prisoners at this bar do not examine the bench. Have you anything further to say in your defence?'

`No, my lord,' said Margery Ashe, and folded her hands again. `I believe the court has it all.'

He looked at the twelve, who sat like men who had been shown a card they were not to look at, and were looking at it; and though the light in the high windows would have served another hour, he adjourned the court.

\scenebreak
\noindent The adjournment occasioned some murmuring, for the county wanted its verdict; Verey gave no reasons. A judge who gives reasons for small things will be asked for them in great ones.

At the lodging the sheriff's people had built the fire high and laid out cold fowl and a Rhenish wine, and Verey ate little and drank less, and afterwards sat long at the table with the depositions squared before him and the day-book, which he had ordered carried up, unopened at his elbow. His weather-glass hung by the window where Harcourt had fixed it the first evening, the quicksilver standing high and steady, promising a fair morning for whatever he should choose to do with it.

Harcourt sat across from him, turning his cup. The boy had been in court all day, in the place kept for the judge's marshal, and had held his tongue through supper by an effort that had been visible from the head of the table, and Verey, who had ridden four circuits with him and was fond of him, at last took pity.

`Say it, Ned.'

`The girl proved the Crown's case, my lord,' Harcourt said. `I know it is not how the court received it. But consider what she swore. Two hundred workings and no failure. If a physician of London could show such a book he would be carried through the streets. And the Serjeant has the right of it: knowing how the thing kills does not make it no killing. Why must a curse be wax and puppets? The sermon Sunday said that God Himself works His will through second causes, through frost and fever and the ordinary instruments of the world. If Providence may use the parish, why may not the Devil? She spoke, and the parish moved, and the man died. That the engine can be described does not prove there is no hand upon it.'

Verey was silent a while. It was the best form of the argument, better than the Serjeant's, and the boy had found it honestly, and it deserved better than a judge's look.

`You argue well,' he said. `And if the question before me were whether something real passed from her door to his, I should be with you, for something did. But that is not the question the statute asks. The statute hangs her if the death was by witchcraft, and what the girl swore is that the means was the parish: Pettit's tap, and Rundle's paper, and the mill, and the coal charity, and forty shut doors, every one of them a lawful act of a free man keeping his own custom. Tell me where in that the felony sits. Not in the doors, for a man may shut his door to whom he pleases. Not in the aggregate, for the law knows no felony of a county. Then in her, who spoke the words? Read the paper, Ned.' He found it among the depositions and pushed it across. `Till he pay what he owes. It is a dun, not a death-warrant. She meant the debt collected. That the parish's engine ground on past the debt and into the ditch is a thing I may lay at many doors in Salt Harrow, but I cannot get intent to kill out of intent to be paid, and without it there is no murder in her though I search until Trinity term. The malice will not carry down the chain. It never does. That is the oldest thing I know in the law.'

`Then leave it to the jury so,' Harcourt said. `Say the evidence does not reach, and let them acquit, and say no more. You need not touch the rest of it.'

`The rest of it,' Verey repeated.

`What the girl said.' The boy leaned forward. `My lord, if you tell the county from the bench that there is nothing in a curse but the telling, you stake the credit of the Queen's justice on a wager. The next man cursed in Essex will sicken or he will not. If he sickens, you are disproved in every alehouse from here to Colchester, and worse than disproved, for they will say the judge tried to talk away the Devil and the Devil answered. Say nothing. The narrow way is safe.'

Verey rose and went to the window. The frost was long gone; the spring mud stood in ruts down the length of the street, shining a little where the lamp over the door caught it, and somewhere below a chain clinked where the javelin-men kept their cold watch. He tapped the glass with one knuckle, an old habit, and the quicksilver shivered and settled where it had stood before. It foretold, and did not make. He had loved the instrument thirty years for exactly that, without once, until tonight, understanding that he had loved it for being innocent, and that its innocence was not his.

`The next man cursed in Essex,' he said, to the window, `will prove whichever of us the county already believes. If they believe the Serjeant, the doors will shut, and the man will sicken, and the book will be marked done, and behold, the curse is proved. If they believe me, the doors will stand open, and the man will thrive, and behold, the judge is proved. That is what the girl put into the world today, Ned, though she does not know it, and I have been all evening trying to find my way around it and there is no way around it. The proof follows the belief. It does not lead it. Whatever the county credits will make itself true in the trying, and the book of it, whichever book, will fill up with honest entries. There is no experiment I can order, no witness I can call, that stands outside the thing and reports on it, because the reporting is the thing.'

Harcourt said, slowly, `Then the narrow way. If it cannot be settled, do not stake the court upon it.'

`The narrow way leaves the engine standing.' Verey came back to the table. He was aware of a great weariness and, beneath it, something with edges, arriving. `I have been reckoning what it does, this engine, since the girl sat down. It collects debts that no bailiff could collect. It keeps knives sheathed that the quarter sessions never see. I do not doubt it is half the government of every parish on this circuit, and cheap at the price, and I understand, Ned, I promise you I understand, what a prudent man leaves standing. But look at what it is. It is a court. She takes a complaint and a fee. She publishes a judgment at the man's own door. The parish executes it, severally, no man answerable, and there is no traverse from it, no jury, no clergy, no pardon, and no appeal, and it hanged Tobias Crane by the roadside over a debt of four pounds without one soul in Salt Harrow doing an unlawful act. The narrow way is not silence, whatever it pretends. Silence from this bench is a licence. And the same belief that turns the engine has other work in this county, Ned: within living memory it has hanged harmless old women by the score, upon evidence of a muttered word and a kept cat; an engine that cannot tell its tradesmen from its scapegoats is the worse court for it, not the better. There cannot be two jurisdictions in one county, and if I leave the shop its credit I have signed its commission.'

`And if you take its credit away,' Harcourt said, `what governs Salt Harrow?'

`The law,' said Verey, and heard how it sounded, and said it again anyway, because the alternative was not saying it. `Worse and slower and dearer. The law.'

The fire had burnt down. Harcourt gathered the cups and, at the door, hesitated in the way he had when he judged his kinsman wrong and could not find the flaw in him, and said goodnight, and went. Verey sat a while longer with the paper that said till he pay what he owes, and the book with its single column, and the thought he had been keeping at arm's length all evening, because it was cold, and because once admitted it would not leave. It was this: that if he went the narrow way, and kept the truth close, and left the county its working belief because it was useful, then he could no longer say what the difference was between his trade and hers. She sold the parish a cause that was not the true cause, because it served. A court that will not name causes truly is only another cunning woman, with a larger parish, and better wine.

He drew a fresh sheet toward him, and mended his pen, and began to set out the heads of his charge to the jury, and the glass by the window stood high and steady all night, promising fair, making nothing.

\scenebreak
\noindent The morning came in fair, as the glass had promised, and the court was fuller than it had been for the trying of the evidence, which Verey had seen before and never liked: a county always finds the judgment better attendance than the proof. They had let the doors stand open for the crowd in the yard, and at the back, at the clerks' table, two shorthand men sat with their books ready, sent down from London, he did not doubt, on the smell of the girl's testimony, which had gone up the road quicker than the post. Whatever he said this morning would be in the Colchester alehouses by Saturday and on the bookstalls by term. He had written his heads out fair in the night, and then, toward dawn, had put the paper in the fire, because a man reading is a man that may be suspected of policy, and what he had to do wanted the other voice, the one the county trusts, the voice of a judge saying only what the law obliges him to say.

The prisoner was set to the bar. She looked, he thought, as she had looked every day, like a tradesman attending a slow reckoning of his own accounts, patient with the arithmetic of other people. The jury took their places, twelve freeholders with the sun on them, and the foreman, a heavy grey man named Osee Gilder who farmed under the Earl at Toppinghall, folded his arms in a manner that Verey had been reading all his life and could not, this morning, read at all. Juries had gone their own way against better judges than he in causes of this colour. He put the thought where he put all thoughts of that kind, behind the business, and began.

`Gentlemen of the jury. The evidence is closed, and it falls to me to lay it before you in order, and to tell you what the law requires of it; and then the matter is yours, and not mine, and no man may take it from you.

`First, then, what stands proved, for much stands proved, and you are not to be told otherwise. It is proved by four witnesses, and not denied by the prisoner, that upon the Thursday before Michaelmas she went to the door of Tobias Crane and there spoke the words you have heard read, before the world, for six shillings had of Nathaniel Dunthorne. It is proved that from that day the man's affairs sickened; that the mill would not take his corn, nor the inn his custom; that his paper was called in; that he was struck from the charity; and that in January, in the great cold that every man of you remembers and some of you will feel in your bones till you die, he was found dead upon the Ardleigh road. A man was cursed at Michaelmas and dead by Candlemas. That is all true, gentlemen, and if the truth of it were the whole of the law, your work were done.

`But the statute of King James, upon which this indictment is laid, does not hang the prisoner for the words, nor for the death, but for the death being wrought by the witchcraft; and there, gentlemen, between the door and the ditch, lies the whole of your inquiry. The Crown must show you the bridge. You are to ask, by what means did the words kill the man?

`You have heard my brother Corse, who has conducted the Crown's case with the learning and the fairness the court expects of him, put before you the judgment at Bury St Edmunds in the late King's time, where my Lord Hale left such a cause to the jury and the jury found the felony. I will tell you plainly how this cause differs. At Bury there was evidence of workings: of acts done, of things found, of fits and visitations sworn to by many. Here the constable and the searchers, sent by the justice with no tender hand, found in the prisoner's house no image, no writing but her book of trade, no instrument of any art but a pen; and the only witness in this cause who could speak to the manner of her workings, her own servant, being sworn, told you what those workings are. She told you, gentlemen, and no witness has gainsaid her, that after the speaking at the door, nothing further is done, because nothing further is needed.

`Attend now to what that nothing is, for the Crown says it is everything, and in a manner the Crown is right. The curse is cried before witnesses, that it be known; and it is known; and thereafter, said the girl, each man in the parish keeps his own door. The miller, for his own reasons, will not grind. The innkeeper, for his own reasons, will not draw. The lender, for his own reasons, and you have all of you met those reasons, calls in his paper. No man is bid. No man conspires. Each does a thing he has a perfect right in law to do, and the sum of those lawful things is that a marked man cannot buy bread among plenty, and goes onto the winter road, and the road, gentlemen, that January, was the coldest in the memory of England, and it killed men in that month who had never been cursed by anybody.

`Now the Serjeant asks you, and it is the strongest thing in his cause, what matter that we can name the engine? A man is as dead by the parish as by a spirit. And I answer him, and this you are to take from me as the law: it matters everything, because the statute requires the means to be the witchcraft, and an act which the law itself allows cannot be a means of murder in the doer of it, and a hundred lawful acts do not make one felony among them, and the law of England knows no way to indict a county. If the parish's shut doors killed Tobias Crane, then you are asked to find that the prisoner killed him by other men's lawful deeds, and to find the malice of murder in a paper whose whole burden, you have heard it read, is till he pay what he owes. That is a creditor's paper, gentlemen. It seeks the debt, not the ditch. The law will not carry a man's purpose down a chain of other men's choosings and call whatever lies at the far end his intent. It has never done so, and it will not begin at this assize.

`And the book. You have heard the book, seventeen years of it, and every working done, and you are asked to take it for the proof of her power. I will tell you what I take it for. Some of you keep a weather-glass, I dare say, as I do; and when the quicksilver falls you carry your hay in, and you are wise to; but no rain ever fell out of the quicksilver, and no man's hay was ever wetted by the glass. Now if the whole county, seeing the glass fall, should not only carry in its hay but shut up its markets, call in its debts, and bar its doors against a neighbour, why then, gentlemen, the falling of the glass would be a calamity indeed, and a book kept of its fallings would show ruin following every one, and the book would be true, and would prove nothing but the county's own obedience. Her book is such a book. It is the ledger of your own doors, gentlemen, and it proves whose hands were on them.

`I say to you therefore: the words are proved, and the death is proved, and between them the Crown has shown you no means but the frost, which is God's, and the parish, which is your own. If, weighing all this, your consciences are satisfied that Tobias Crane died by witchcraft, you will do your duty and find her guilty, and let no fear of the world move you. But if you cannot be so satisfied, if the bridge from the door to the ditch will not bear the weight the Crown lays on it, then your duty is as plain the other way, and you will find her not guilty, and let no fear of the Devil move you either. Consider of your verdict.'

The court held still. Verey sat back, and became aware, in the manner of a man surfacing, of the particulars of the room: the scratching of the shorthand men, racing each other to the end of him; the yard beyond the doors, silent, which meant every word had carried; Serjeant Corse, looking at the table before him, an old man who had been ready all his life for a judgment day of one kind, and had been given the other kind. In the gallery Dunthorne the maltster wore the inward squint of a man moving figures from one column to another and liking the new totals less. The miller studied his own hands as though they had been handed to him that morning, a stranger's goods, dear at the price.

The jury did not retire. Gilder leaned to his left, and heads bent together along the two rows in the box, some briefly, some at length; one man, at the far end, held out longer than the rest, his mouth set, and Verey watched Gilder work upon him with nothing but the patient sideways attitude of a farmer waiting out weather, and thought: there is the county, in one box, doing in one minute what it will do in every parish by Whitsun. Then the heads came apart.

The clerk of assize rose. `Gentlemen, are you agreed of your verdict?'

`We are.'

`How say you: is Margery Ashe guilty of the felony and witchcraft whereof she stands indicted, or not guilty?'

`Not guilty.'

The yard learnt it before the gallery had finished drawing breath; Verey heard the word go out of the open doors and over the crowd like wind going over standing corn, and away down the street, and he thought, with neither triumph nor comfort, that no rider in England could have carried it so fast, and that it would be in Salt Harrow by nightfall, ahead of any of the witnesses, in the one conveyance that pays no toll. At the bar, Margery Ashe had not moved. She was looking at him, and her face held the same grave mourning it had held for the girl, and Verey understood that he had this morning done to her trade, from the bench and gratis, precisely what she had done to Tobias Crane at his own front door for six shillings; and that she was the one soul in the county who knew it.

`Let the prisoner be discharged,' he said.

\partheading{III}{The Summer Assizes}
\noindent The circuit brought him back through the county in July, in the dust and the leather smell of high summer, and the same bells, and the same javelin-men sweating in their liveries, and at the sheriff's table that evening the story was served to him before the fish.

`You will not have heard, my lord, being upon the western roads,' the sheriff said, filling his glass with the ceremony of a man presenting a gift. `We have had a miracle in Salt Harrow. A failed curse. The first, they will tell you, in living memory, though how a memory keeps the failures I am sure I cannot say.' He laughed at his own wit, and the table with him. `A drover there, one of the Aldous family, fell behind with a butcher over winter feed, and got himself cried at his own door in the old style, the whole form of it, witnesses and all. And the next market day the man is at his standing, buying and selling like a Christian, and the market, my lord, did not know what to do with him. They say you could watch men remember and forget in the same moment. By the second week he had his price as good as any man, and the butcher is paid off since, in the ordinary dull way of money, and that,' the sheriff raised his glass, `is the estate of witchcraft in this county since Lent, and the county knows whose doing it is. Your lordship's charge has been through three printings. They cried it at the market cross in Salt Harrow, I am told, off a broadside, the Saturday after the assize.'

`The same cross,' Verey said, `where they cried the curse.'

`The very same, I should think. There is but the one cross.' The sheriff, pleased, turned to the business of the fish, and the talk went elsewhere, and Verey sat with his glass and let it.

It was Harcourt, later, in the cool of the lodging with the windows open to the summer dark, who brought him the rest of it, in the level voice the boy had begun using with him since March, the voice of a man rendering accounts he would sooner not have kept.

`She has left the county, my lord. Ashe. Gave up the shop at Lady Day and removed into the fen country, Ely way or beyond; the girl went with her. They say she does a good trade there.' He paused. `They say it without wonder, my lord, as a thing that stands to reason. It seems your lordship's charge travels as far as the printers carry it, and no further. Past the reach of the broadside the old trade is what it always was.'

`And within its reach?'

`Within its reach, the quarter sessions are up by half. Debt, most of it, the small ugly kind, and a matter of two affrays at Salt Harrow since Easter, which the justices there tell me is two more than the parish has furnished in ten years. Rundle the lender has taken a man to prison for four pounds. The wardens have had the constable at the alehouse twice.' Harcourt stood at the window, not looking at him. `It is as you said it would be, my lord. Worse, and slower, and dearer. I set it out fair because you would want it fair.'

`I would,' said Verey. `Thank you, Ned.'

He sat up a while after the boy had gone. On the wall by the window the weather-glass hung where it always hung, and he found he had been listening for the small sounds of the town below as a man listens to a ward at night, for what is absent in it.

\scenebreak
\noindent Market day fell before the commission opened, and Verey's lodging stood above the square, and in the morning he took his coffee at the window, which he would have called idleness in another man.

Harcourt found the drover for him without being asked, the way he now anticipated most things: a broad sunburnt fellow in a blue coat, standing at the sheep pens with his hand on a hurdle, talking prices. Verey watched him a long time. There was nothing to see, and he understood that the nothing was the whole spectacle: men came to the pens, and met the drover's eye, and some of them hung an instant on the meeting, the way a man's foot hangs above a stair that was once broken and is mended, and then they took his hand, and haggled, and paid. A woman sold him bread. A carter took up his fleeces. At the market cross a boy was crying a broadside, some fresher scandal, a London poisoning, and no one about the pens paid the crying any mind at all; that had been the sound of ruin in this county from before any of them was born, and it was only news now, and Verey, watching the drover tell his money over in the open sun, thought that he had never in thirty years seen an acquittal walk about a market before, and that it was a better sight than he deserved.

Then he saw Dunthorne.

The maltster stood in his own doorway across the square, in his apron, as a man stands who has come out for no purpose he could name if asked. He was not watching the drover. He was watching, with a tradesman's face that gave out nothing, the men who took the drover's hand; and after a while he turned his head and said something to two fellows who stood inside his shadow, big, idle-standing men that Verey did not know and knew perfectly well, having seen the pattern of them at every assize of his life, in the dock, mostly, at the far end of exactly such mornings as this. Dunthorne spoke, and the two men detached themselves from the doorway and went off up the lane toward the Salt Harrow road, unhurried, in step, and the maltster looked after them for a moment and then went into his shop, and the door shut, and the square went on being a market.

Verey stood at the window with his coffee going cold. Below him the drover laughed at something, a big easy laugh that carried over the pens, a man restored to the ordinary hazards of the world; and Verey found that he could put no name to what he felt that would not shame either the truth or himself, because both things in the square were true at once and neither would give way: he had told the county the truth about causes, and there it stood in a blue coat, counting its money in the sun; and the county had begun, in a doorway across the square, its own quiet inquiry into what the lie had been holding up. Behind him on the wall the quicksilver was falling. Foul weather coming, the glass said, in its old innocent office, foretelling and making nothing; and Verey stood at the window until his coffee was done, keeping, for as long as the morning lasted, an eye on the weather he had made.
